Envelopes ​

An envelope is how a note rises and falls: the swell of a bowed string, the sharp hit and quick fade of a pluck, the long glow of a pad. Every built-in instrument has three, on the Envelopes tab: Env 1 shapes the loudness, Env 2 sweeps the filter through its Env amount, and Env 3 is wired to nothing, free for whatever the Mod Matrix sends it to, such as the pitch for a falling tom or a laser zap. The Mod Matrix can send Env 1 and Env 2 anywhere else too.

At the foot of Env 2's column, Disconnect filter, off unless switched on, unhooks Env 2 from the filter: the cutoff then stays where the Filter tab sets it, and Env 2 is free for the Mod Matrix to send elsewhere, such as to the pulse width or to an LFO's depth through a Via.

Swappable Envelopes ​

Swappable envelopes are a key part of the synths' design. Most synthesizers have one kind of envelope and only let you change its times. Here each of the three envelope slots holds an envelope of any type, and the types are not variations of one design: each is its own circuit, with its own way of moving and its own settings. A chip voice can fade in its chip's own steps or be given a smooth ADSR; an acid bass can keep its accent gate or be struck through a vactrol, whose light-sensitive cell falls unevenly and remembers the last hit; a pad can swell with a slow curved attack. Changing an envelope's type changes how an instrument feels to play as much as changing its oscillator, and the envelopes are where many of the engines' characters live, so mixing one engine's oscillator with another's envelope is how the synths' strangest and most personal sounds are found.

Every envelope works its level out from the time into its note, in one formula, rather than by stepping from one sample to the next, so that a note sounds the same whichever chunk of sound it falls in, live and in a WAV export alike.

Every envelope can be very snappy and tight, yet none clicks: the quickest rise takes half a millisecond and the quickest fall two, as quick as the snappiest analog envelopes, so that a time of 0 ms is as short as a note can start or stop without a tick. A vactrol falls in no less than four milliseconds, and Chip Steps ease in their first level over the same half millisecond.

ADSR ​

The envelope of most synthesizers. It rises over its Attack, stays at the top for its Hold, falls over its Decay to its Sustain while the note is held, and falls from wherever it is over its Release once the note ends. On an instrument of one voice, a note struck while the last still sounds starts its attack from where the envelope had reached, keeping the oscillators running, so that striking again never clicks.

The Curve sets the attack's shape. Above zero it is the charge of a capacitor in an analog envelope circuit: the level chases a target set above full, so that it rises fast and slows as it nears the top, reaching full on time; the higher the curve, the nearer the target and the more bowed the rise. At zero the rise is a straight ramp. Below zero it is an exponential swell, slow at first and rushing at the end, as a hand pushes a fader up. The decay and the release are exponential falls, as a capacitor discharges, each set by how long the fall takes to become inaudible. Snap mixes in a second, much faster fall at the start of the decay, which gives a hit its click without shortening its body.

AR ​

A strike: the envelope rises over its Attack and falls over its Release at once, however long the note is held, as a drum or a plucked string does. A note's velocity sets how hard it is struck.

ASR ​

An organ's envelope: it rises over its Attack, holds at full for as long as the note is held, and falls over its Release once it ends.

SID ADSR ​

The envelope of an 8-bit sound chip, as the chip makes it. Each of its four settings is a step of the chip's own table rather than a time, and the field shows the time each step stands for. The level is a counter: the attack counts it up at a steady rate; the decay and the release count it down through an exponential counter that is divided into segments, each slower than the last, so that the fall is a chain of straight lines bending towards silence, the chip's way of faking an exponential curve. The sustain is one of the chip's sixteen levels, and the level heard is always the counter's whole steps, so a slow fade has the chip's faint staircase.

Vactrol ​

A vactrol is a light and a light-sensitive cell sealed together, used in the low-pass gates of modular synthesizers. A strike lights the light, and the cell's conductance rises towards it, faster the brighter it is lit; when the light goes out, the cell falls in a hyperbolic curve, quick at first and then lingering, to a tenth over the cell's fall time, with a slow leak under its tail, so that a strike is a pluck with a body, as loud to the ear as a note of any other envelope. Because the rise depends on how brightly it is lit, a hard strike is snappy and a soft one lingers, so no two hits sound alike.

Response is how fast the cell is, from fast at 0% to lazy at 100%. Trigger is a short flash at each note, lasting its Trigger time, or the Gate, lit for as long as the note is held. Memory is how much of the cell's last glow carries into the next hit, so that hits close together build on each other, as a real cell's do. Age dims the light and slows the cell, as an old one does. Stability is how alike each hit is: below full, each hit draws its own small differences in rise, fall and brightness from a seeded random source, the same at every playing, and the room's slowly changing warmth drifts them further.

The cell also remembers its light over a longer time, as a real one does. Kept lit, it tires within a second or so and answers each strike more slowly, up to twice as slowly; left dark for a minute or more, it rests and is quick again. A passage played hard softens its attacks as it goes on, and the first notes after a rest snap. A gated note tires the cell for as long as it is held.

Acid Gate ​

The envelope of an acid bass line. Each note opens quickly over its Soft attack and decays over its Amp decay. A note at Accent at's velocity or louder is accented: it opens at once, swells for a moment by its Accent level, and gives the filter a short extra sweep that falls over its Accent decay. Behind the accent is a capacitor, charged a little by each accented note, in proportion to the filter's resonance and the Accent sweep, and draining between notes; a run of accents close together charges it further, so the filter climbs higher with each one, the squelch of an acid line.

Its Circuit is Stock or Modded. Stock plays the circuit's own accent decay, accent sweep, amp decay and soft attack. Modded shows them, as the modifications players made to the original machine let them be set.

Chip Steps ​

The volume envelope of a 4-bit chip, which has no smooth curves at all. The level starts at its Start level, one of sixteen steps, and at each tick of its Level pace moves one step down, or up, by Level goes. Held, it stays where it starts. A note's end fades it out very quickly, so that it doesn't click.

Off ​

Env 2 and Env 3 can be set to Off, which holds them at nothing: Env 2 then sweeps no filter, and the Mod Matrix reads nothing from either. With Env 2 Off and the Filter set to (none), you hear the oscillator as it is, a quick way to learn what an oscillator sounds like. Env 1 is the loudness, so it always holds an envelope.

Planned ​

The agreed design adds a fourth envelope, and two inputs to the gate types: a low-pass gate envelope, which brings its own low-pass filter and amp following the vactrol's response, and a strike and a gate that the Mod Matrix can route to either.
