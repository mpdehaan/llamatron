On this page

Synths ​

llamatron has synthesizers of its own, so that a set can play with no DAW, no hardware and no SoundFont. A built-in instrument is a semi-modular synth, inspired equally by traditional monosynths, grooveboxes and eurorack modules, in a fusion of the three that is mostly never seen before. Like a monosynth, it is a voice you can play straight away, its parts already wired the way a player expects. Like a groovebox, it comes with engines that each start as a finished sound, chip voices and an acid bass and a drum machine among them, ready for a pattern. Like a rack of modules, every part of it can be pulled out and replaced by another: an acid bass's oscillator can play through a vactrol and a low-pass gate, and a chip voice through a ladder filter.

Choosing a Synth ​

On the Instruments page, open an instrument and set its Kind to Built-in. The Engine field under it then chooses the synth, and the tabs below hold its parts.

An engine is a starting point, a preset of every part: choosing one fills the Oscillators, Envelopes, Filter, Modulators, FX and Mod Matrix tabs as that synth has them. Each part has a Type, and any part can then be swapped for another type, even one another engine starts with, so that the engines are places to start rather than walls. A part's settings are only the ones its type has, so an ADSR shows its Attack, Decay, Sustain and Release, and a vactrol its Cell, Memory, Age and Stability.

Engines ​

The engines come in two lists. A standard engine plays notes, for a lead, a bass, a pad or a chord. A drum engine plays hits, for a percussion instrument, each note tuning the drum. Engines marked planned are agreed and not built yet; their pages say so.

How a Voice Is Wired ​

Each note is a voice, run one sample at a time. Its oscillator makes the tone, its pitch moved by the glide and by the Mod Matrix. The filter takes part of the tone away, its cutoff moved by Env 2 through the filter's Env amount, by key tracking and by the Mod Matrix. The amp sets the loudness, from Env 1 and the note's velocity, moved by the Mod Matrix. These routes are wired in, as a semi-modular synth's normalled connections are, so that every slot of the Mod Matrix stays free for movement of your own. The instrument's voices are then added together and pass through its effects in order.

A shared filter is the exception to "each note its own": the voices are added first, and the one filter, moved by the newest note, takes their sum. A low-pass gate is always each note's own, since it is that note's amp as well as its filter.

How the Sound Is Made ​

The synths run in llamatron's backend, apart from the pages the tracker draws, on threads of their own, so that a page being drawn never makes a note late; Backend describes it in full. The sound reaches the sound card through cpal, a Rust library for audio output: CoreAudio on macOS, ALSA on Linux and WASAPI on Windows. llamatron is built and played mostly on macOS, so a problem with sound on Linux or Windows is most likely in the sound card's driver or cpal's support for it; the backend writes what it found at start-up, and any problem, to rotating log files in ~/.llamatron/.

The audio callback, which the sound card calls for each chunk, only plays: everything an instrument needs is made when the instrument is compiled, on another thread, and the callback allocates nothing and waits on no lock. Notes and changes reach it through lock-free queues. MIDI and FluidSynth are held back by the moment the sound takes to reach the speakers, so that every instrument lands together.

Whatever a patch does, the built-in sound protects ears and speakers: a part that runs away is silenced and starts again clean, the lowest rumble and the highest hash are taken out, and a limiter keeps the loudest peaks just under full scale; Ctrl+T silences everything.
