# Television Themes

Two families: the family comedy's opening song and the adventure series'
orchestral theme. Principles only. Much of what can be read about them is fan
writing, so most of this is practice and judgment, and thin: to be deepened
before either is built.

## The Family Comedy Theme (1980s)

- **What it is for**: a whole song in under a minute, heard every week, that
  says the show is warm and safe. Memorable first, and nostalgic by design: it
  should sound like looking at a photo album. (practice)
- **Shape**: no intro to speak of (a pickup, a fill or one bar), a short verse
  of 8 bars, then the chorus with the title or the show's idea in it, and a
  tag: the last line again, slower or held, with a closing flourish. 45 to 70
  seconds; a 20 to 30 second cut for later seasons is the chorus and the tag
  alone. Nothing may wait: the hook arrives within 15 seconds. (practice)
- **Sound**: adult contemporary soft rock. Electric piano or a bell-like
  digital piano carrying the chords, a clean guitar with chorus, a round
  fretless or synth bass, light drums with gated reverb and a side stick in
  the verse, strings or a soft pad behind, and a saxophone, harmonica or lead
  guitar taking the fills and the last word. (practice)
- **Voices**: one warm singer, or a man and a woman trading lines and joining
  in harmony on the chorus; stacked harmonies on the title. Where the theme is
  instrumental, a saxophone or a whistled or piano tune takes the singer's
  place. (practice)
- **Tune**: singable by a family, in a major key, mostly steps, within an
  octave and a bit, the title on the longest and highest notes, each phrase
  answered by the next. The chorus's last line climbs to a held note.
  (practice)
- **Chords**: major keys; I, IV, V, vi and ii, with added ninths and major
  sevenths for warmth, a IV over V before each arrival, slash chords with a
  walking or falling bass, and often a key change up a step or a semitone for
  the last line. (practice)
- **Tempo and feel**: medium, 90 to 125, straight eighths or a gentle shuffle;
  a few are slow ballads. (practice)
- **The other kinds of the decade**, for contrast: the funk and jazz
  instrumental with slap bass and saxophone; the solo piano and voice that
  opens small and swells; the ironic use of an old standard. (practice)

## How the Family Comedy Themes Were Made

- **Written by pop songwriters, not score composers**: a tunesmith and a
  lyricist as a pair, often with radio hits of their own, so the theme is a
  real pop single cut short; several were released as singles and charted.
  Session players of jazz fusion and soft rock wrote and played others, which
  is where the electric piano, the clean guitar and the saxophone come from.
  (practice)
- **The hook first**: the title line and its tune were written before anything
  else, often by the lyricist, from one sentence about what the show is about;
  the rest of the song was built to arrive at that line. (practice)
- **Written fast, from the pilot**: one idea, one mood, no second thoughts. A
  theme has one job and one hook. (practice)
- **One team, many shows**: a pair who wrote for one production company gave
  that company's shows a house sound: the same tempo band, the same climbing
  chorus, the same singer. A wizard's style may be this narrow on purpose.
  (judgment)
- **Versions**: the same song was sung by a new singer, as a solo and then as
  a duet, re-recorded with newer instruments, or cut from a minute to twenty
  seconds as seasons passed. Some shows played the tune without words at the
  top and sang it at the end. So a theme has a long cut, a short cut and an
  instrumental cut of one tune. (practice)
- **Borrowed songs**: some themes were pop songs written years earlier, chosen
  because their title said the show's idea. (practice)
- **A full-length version** of a minute-long theme adds a second verse and a
  bridge that the broadcast never played. (practice)

## Measured: A Dozen Family Comedy Themes

From published sheet music markings and community chord charts of about a
dozen themes, 1978 to 1989; the charts disagree with each other by a key or a
few beats a minute, so these are bands, not points. No tune was read.
(measured, thin)

- **Key**: major in every one. No theme of the family kind is in a minor key.
- **Tempo, two bands**: the ballads at 79 to 100 (about a third of them), and
  the upbeat ones at 115 to 145, most near 120. Nothing between 100 and 115.
  The latest and brightest sit highest (140 and over).
- **Feel**: straight eighths in most; a shuffle or a medium swing in about a
  quarter, the swing ones marked in two.
- **Chords, the plain kind**: I, IV, V and vi or ii, four chords for the whole
  theme, in half of them.
- **Chords, the warm kind** (the ballads): sevenths throughout, ii7 and vi7,
  the major II as a dominant leading to V, slash chords with a moving bass.
- **Chords, the borrowed kind**: bVII as a fourth chord beside I, IV and V in
  the rock-leaning ones; diminished passing chords climbing by semitone
  between IV and V and between V and vi in the swing ones.
- **The bridge of the long cut** goes to the flat side: bVI, bIII or the minor
  iv, then back by V. The broadcast cut drops it.
- **Sections of the long cut**: intro, verse, pre-chorus, chorus, interlude,
  bridge, chorus, outro or fade. The broadcast cut is intro, verse, chorus,
  tag, or chorus and tag alone; early seasons ran up to 90 seconds, later ones
  30 or less.
- **The verse worries, the chorus reassures**: a low, plain, almost lamenting
  verse and a soaring chorus is a stated design in more than one. The lift is
  range and voices, not volume: one voice stacked several times on the chorus
  stands in for a group.
- **Small bands**: the most loved are sparse, piano and voice with little
  else; a few instruments, kept to a minimum.
- **An opening figure**: a short instrumental signature before the voice (two
  chords rocked on a piano, an old-time piano flourish, a guitar figure), 2 to
  4 bars, recognizable alone.
- **Rearranged, not rewritten**: one instrumental theme was dressed anew
  nearly every season (small-group jazz, Latin, voices alone, orchestra,
  saxophone rhythm and blues, hip hop with trumpet) with the tune unchanged. A
  theme is a tune plus a dress.
- **Singers by color**: a country-pop tenor, a rasping blues-rock voice, a
  smooth crooner with a soprano; the singer's color places the show as much as
  the chords do.

## The Old-Time Kind

A family comedy theme written new in the manner of popular song of the 1900s
to 1920s, for charm and gentle irony. To discuss with the user as a style of
its own.

- **Manner**: a gravelly, relaxed, half-spoken croon, behind the beat;
  fingerpicked guitar with a ragtime bounce (an alternating thumb bass under
  syncopated treble), or stride piano; clarinet answering the voice; tuba or
  string bass on beats one and three; banjo or brushed snare on two and four;
  a muted trumpet or violin for color. (practice)
- **Feel**: a two-beat, bass on one and three and the chord after, at an easy
  walking tempo; swung eighths. (practice)
- **Chords**: I vi ii V turnarounds, chains of dominants round the circle of
  fifths (III7 VI7 II7 V7), secondary dominants before every arrival, a
  diminished chord passing between I and ii or IV and V, the minor iv before
  home. (practice)
- **Form**: a 16- or 32-bar chorus, A A B A, the title in the first or last
  line of each A, the B a run of dominants; an introductory verse is optional
  and usually dropped; a 2- or 4-bar tag repeats the last line. (measured)
- **Endings**: a stock closing figure, a held chord with a clarinet run, or a
  ritard on the tag. A later season may dress the same tune as a street-parade
  band or as a smoother small-group jazz. (practice)

## The Adventure Series Theme

See `SOUNDTRACK.md`, "The Adventure Score": the television theme is that score
in 60 to 90 seconds. To research when reached: the opening fanfare over a
spoken or silent teaser, the broad tune in horns over a march or a pulsing
ostinato, the softer second strain, the return and a final held chord; and the
later, darker kind built on drums, voices and a slow modal tune.
