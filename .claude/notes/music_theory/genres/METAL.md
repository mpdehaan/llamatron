# Metal

What metal does apart from hard rock, and two of its kinds in depth.
Principles only; measures are from the chord dataset described in
`HARMONY.md`. The polished eighties kind, nu metal and grunge are in
`ROCK.md`.

## What Marks Metal

- **Minor is home**: six songs in ten or more are minor (hard rock one in
  three; the melodic and power kinds eight in ten). The loops are Aeolian: i
  bVII, i bVI, i bVI bVII, the cadence bVI bVII i, and i bVI bIII bVII in
  choruses. No sevenths at all: the chord is the root and fifth, and color
  comes from the riff's single notes. (measured)
- **Borrowed majors** where a song is major: bVII in over half, bIII in four
  in ten, the major II in six in ten. A flat second (Phrygian) and a flat
  fifth (the tritone, the blues scale's or the diminished) are the dark colors
  in riffs. (measured)
- **The riff is the song**: sections are named by their riffs, not their
  chords; a song has three to six riffs. The chord count of a song is the
  highest of any style (110 and more changes) because riffs move every beat.
- **Shape**: intro in more than eight songs in ten, the highest of all; verse,
  chorus, verse, chorus, solo, chorus the commonest shape; a solo in half to
  three quarters of songs; an interlude in a third to four in ten (a clean or
  slow passage, or a new riff section); eight or nine sections. (measured)
- **Palm muting** separates riff notes that chug from chords that ring;
  down-picking for weight, alternate picking for speed.

## Galloping Heavy Metal

The British kind from around 1980: melodic, fast, bright in sound and minor in
key. A solo in three songs in four and an intro in more than nine in ten, both
the highest of any style measured; an interlude in four in ten. (measured,
practice)

- **The gallop**: an eighth and two sixteenths on each beat (or its reverse,
  two sixteenths and an eighth), on the low home note, palm-muted, by bass and
  both guitars together, with power chords changing on top. At speed it is the
  engine of whole songs; a triplet version at slower tempos. The bass plays it
  with the fingers, loud and bright in the mix, and is the lead rhythm
  instrument: the guitars lock to the bass, not the other way round.
- **The bass leads**: melodic runs between chords, chords and open strings
  ringing, a line that states the tune in the intro before the guitars; in
  quiet passages the bass carries the harmony with arpeggios.
- **Two guitars in harmony**: a tune played by one guitar and doubled by the
  other a diatonic third above (thirds mostly, with fourths and fifths where a
  third would clash, which gives a medieval color), same rhythm, same bends.
  The harmonized tune is a hook as strong as the chorus and often stands where
  a chorus would: after the sung chorus, as the intro, and between verses.
  First stated by one guitar, then doubled, then harmonized.
- **Harmony**: natural minor (Aeolian), often with E or A as home; i bVI bVII
  and bVI bVII i everywhere, the three chords a whole song can be built on; i
  bVII bVI bVII; a move to the relative major for a chorus; Phrygian color for
  menace; the harmonic minor's major V for a classical turn. Roots move by
  step more than by fourth.
- **Drums**: one bass drum played fast with one foot, not two; the gallop
  echoed on the kick or left to the bass while the drums play a driving
  straight beat; the ride cymbal's bell or a half-open hi-hat for the fast
  passages; fills round many toms into each section; a jazz-trained looseness,
  the feel pushing forward. Time changes are marked by the drums alone for a
  bar.
- **Shape**: an intro that is its own piece (a clean arpeggio, a bass tune or
  a harmonized melody, often slow, then the tempo doubles); verse and chorus
  twice; then a long instrumental middle of several riffs: a new riff, the
  harmonized tune, a first solo by one guitarist, a second solo by the other
  over different chords, a tempo or meter change (to half-time, to triplets,
  to a march), a rebuild; then the last chorus, and the intro again to close
  the circle. Long songs are arches: what opens returns only at the end, with
  episodes between that do not repeat. Six to eight minutes is common;
  thirteen is possible.
- **Solos**: two, one from each guitarist, contrasting (one fluid and legato,
  one sharper and blues-based), each eight or sixteen bars, over a riff that
  changes chord every bar or two; then both in harmony.
- **The voice**: high, operatic, long held notes above the gallop, the tune
  stepping through the minor scale; choruses made to be sung by a crowd, often
  wordless vowels on the harmonized guitar tune.
- **Tempo and feel**: 130 to 200; almost never swung except by the triplet
  gallop; no keyboards, or only held pads later.

## Groove Metal

The American kind from around 1990: slower than thrash, heavier, and built on
rhythm. Minor in two songs in three; a solo in a third; a bridge in nearly
half; pop's shape (verse, chorus, verse, chorus, bridge or solo, chorus) more
than metal's. (measured, practice)

- **The power groove**: mid tempo (90 to 130), a syncopated palm-muted riff of
  one to three pitches whose accents fall across the bar line, with rests as
  heavy as the notes. The drums are glued to the guitar riff note for note,
  kick with every pick stroke; the bass doubles the guitar. Everything starts
  from the groove: the drummer lays back, and even the fast passages return to
  it.
- **Riff vocabulary**: the low string tuned down a step or more; chromatic
  power chords crawling by semitone around the home chord; the tritone and the
  blues scale's flat fifth; bends on low notes that make the riff snarl;
  pinched harmonic squeals at the ends of phrases; tight sixteenth-note bursts
  like a machine, then a held chord; riffs of three against the drums' four;
  triplet and twelve-eight leanings from blues and southern boogie under the
  weight.
- **One guitarist as two**: with no second guitar, the player throws high
  melodic stabs and low chromatic chord runs into the gaps of the main riff,
  so that it sounds like an answer from another player. Under the solo there
  is no rhythm guitar: the bass alone holds the riff, distorted and exact, and
  the texture thins on purpose.
- **Drums**: two bass drums in bursts that double the riff's sixteenths, never
  constant; a bright clicking kick that cuts through; the backbeat simple and
  behind; china and crash accents on the riff's pushes; no blast beats: fills
  are short and return to the groove.
- **Harmony**: i bVI, i bIII, i bVII as the slow motion under the riffs; many
  riffs are one chord with chromatic neighbors and have no progression;
  choruses on i bVI bIII bVII or simply the riff with a shouted hook.
- **Solos**: minor pentatonic and blues scale with wide vibrato, whammy-bar
  dives and harmonics screamed up by the bar, fast legato runs, then a tune;
  sometimes a nod to Mixolydian or Dorian for a southern color.
- **The voice**: shouted or barked in rhythm with the riff, a rough tune only
  in choruses; the title chanted.
- **Shape**: a riff intro, often the drums alone first; verse on the main
  groove; a pre-chorus riff; chorus; a breakdown at half the speed where the
  heaviest riff arrives; a solo; the last chorus; an ending on the breakdown
  riff slowing to a stop.

## The Power Ballad

A style of its own, shared by hard rock, hair metal and arena rock. (practice)

- Slow (60 to 90), in 4/4 or 12/8. It opens with one instrument and the voice:
  clean arpeggiated guitar with a chorus effect, piano, or acoustic guitar.
  The first verse has no drums; they enter at the second verse or the first
  chorus, often with one big fill. Distorted guitars arrive at the chorus as
  long held chords, not riffs.
- Major or its relative minor; I V vi IV, vi IV I V, I IV, and a falling bass
  under a held chord; a pre-chorus that climbs.
- Each chorus is bigger than the last: harmony voices stacked, strings or a
  keyboard pad, the drums fuller.
- A guitar solo that sings the tune, over the chorus's chords, ending on a
  high held bend into the last chorus.
- The last chorus often a step higher, repeated to a fade or to a quiet ending
  by the opening instrument alone.
