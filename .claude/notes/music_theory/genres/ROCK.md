# Rock

Guitars, bass and drums with a backbeat; the riff and the chorus are the song.
Verse and chorus forms of eight-bar sections, a bridge or a solo late. Major
and minor keys with the flat seventh chord borrowed freely. (practice)

- **Rock and roll**: twelve-bar or three-chord songs, fast, a shuffle or a
  straight eighth feel, a drum fill in, a hard stop.
- **British invasion**: tight songs, sung harmonies, a counted or filled
  start, clear endings; chords richer than three.
- **Folk rock**: strummed or picked guitar first, light drums, the lead
  instrument sparing; held endings.
- **Hard rock**: the riff first, on guitar, then drums and bass together;
  heavy backbeat, big fills, a big finish.
- **Arena rock**: built in layers toward a large chorus; the hook stated
  before the verse; solos; a big finish.
- **Punk**: fast, counted in or straight in, everyone at once, few fills, no
  ghost notes, a hard stop, hardly a solo.
- **New wave**: drums and bass or keys first, tight and dry, eighth-note bass,
  synth hooks; held or riff endings.
- **Rock ballad**: keys or a clean guitar alone first, the band entering at a
  chorus, slowing at the end.

## Ways of Playing Rock, Beyond the Subgenres

Each is a set of habits that could become a subgenre or settings of one.
(practice, from lessons and analyses of the styles)

- **Clean and conversational**: a clean guitar played with the fingers,
  answering every sung line with a short fill of chord tones, double stops and
  slides; dynamics from touch, quiet verses, the rhythm section light and
  steady, a long melodic solo at the end that builds from the fills already
  heard. The voice half-spoken. Folk, country and jazz chords inside rock.
- **Slow and spacious**: tempos far below other rock, near 60 to 70; a chord
  held for many bars, sometimes most of a minute; long instrumental
  introductions that add one part at a time; sections that would fill a short
  song stretched by repetition into a long one; a slow, singing guitar solo of
  few notes with long bends; keyboards holding pads; whole songs joined into
  suites.
- **Heavy blues, light and shade**: a riff of one or two bars in guitar and
  bass together, with holes of silence that the drums fill; drums huge, behind
  the beat, with triplet figures on the kick; riffs that turn against the bar
  while the drums keep four; loud electric sections set against quiet acoustic
  ones in the same song, in steps rather than slopes.
- **Swinging hard rock**: the riff is a shuffle or a boogie at high speed,
  swung about 60 percent, drums from big-band swing; rhythm guitar full of
  small fills between chords; bright, major, playful; a short flashy solo;
  backing voices high above.
- **Two guitars, one loose and one tight**: a rhythm guitar tight, simple and
  punk-like low down, a lead guitar bluesy and singing above, the two rarely
  playing the same thing; a clean arpeggio figure as an intro hook; long outro
  solos.
- **Southern and twin lead**: two lead guitars playing the tune together a
  third or a sixth apart, moving through the scale so the interval is
  sometimes major and sometimes minor; major and minor pentatonic mixed; two
  drummers playing different, interlocking parts so the rhythm section is a
  lead voice; long instrumentals; an organ.
- **The southern epic**: a slow ballad, organ or piano and slide guitar,
  verses and choruses complete in four or five minutes; then the tempo jumps
  to nearly double and the band plays a long outro over a short repeating loop
  of three chords, guitars soloing in turn and then together in harmony,
  rising to the end. The fast part began as a way to end the song and grew. A
  related shape keeps one tempo: song, long themed solo, song, longer solo as
  a call and response between two guitars panned left and right, building to
  the fastest playing at the end.
- **The guitar army**: three guitars, two trading and harmonizing leads while
  one holds rhythm, with three- and four-part vocal harmonies from country
  rock and fast, clean, country and bluegrass picking in the leads.

## More Ways of Playing Rock

Habits gathered style by style; each could be a subgenre or the defaults of
one. (practice, from lessons, reviews and interviews; thin where marked)

- **Pop punk**: 160 to 200 and faster; power chords and barre chords
  downpicked in even eighths, palm-muted in verses and opened in choruses; the
  bass is the melodic one, walking and running between roots, and may carry a
  verse alone before the guitars crash in; major keys, three or four chords,
  short songs. Some songs swing their eighths. The verse thin, the chorus
  full.
- **Emo and melodic punk**: the same engine with a second guitar playing
  octave leads over the chords in choruses and bridges, singable and never
  showy; clean arpeggios in verses; two or three guitar parts, not a wall, so
  the pick attack stays clear; quiet bridges that build; acoustic or country
  colors now and then.
- **Metal-leaning pop punk**: adds galloping palm-muted riffs, harmonized
  leads and a real solo from thrash.
- **Ska punk**: guitar chops on every off-beat, short and percussive; horns
  (trumpet, trombone, sax) that double the hook, stab the off-beats or play a
  countermelody; bass busy and fast rather than walking; drums switch between
  an off-beat ska feel in verses and straight double-time punk in choruses.
  The switch is the form.
- **Power pop**: melody first, chords found after; major-key power chords
  under a sweet tune with a sad story; the four chord leaned on; short songs,
  a hook every few bars, wordless vocal hooks, a key change or a borrowed
  minor chord for a pang.
- **Nineties radio rock**: grunge's loud guitars with pop construction; mid
  tempo, earnest, a clean or acoustic verse and a wall in the chorus; open
  tunings for ringing, unusual chords; half-spoken rhythmic verses in the
  lighter kind.
- **Quiet and loud**: a hushed verse and an explosive chorus, the drums doing
  most of the lifting; stops and starts.
- **Layered alternative**: many guitar tracks stacked until they are an
  orchestra, polished, with a jazz-trained drummer whose rolls and ghost notes
  keep the wall moving; dreamy quiet passages against the wall.
- **Progressive rock**: long forms in sections rather than verses and
  choruses; meters of five and seven and changes of meter, tempo and key; a
  lead bass that plays counter-lines high and bright; imitation between
  instruments suggesting counterpoint; modes rather than chord function; a
  violin or keyboards as a second lead; in the American kind, boogie rock
  underneath.
- **Modern heavy prog**: odd meters (five, seven, eleven) written around the
  drummer, soft atmospheric verses with harmony vocals, then a heavy riff;
  long builds.
- **Groove metal**: mid tempo; a syncopated palm-muted riff locked to the kick
  drum note for note, bass with them; rests as heavy as notes; triplet and
  twelve-eight lean; a riff of three against the drums' four; beats displaced
  by a sixteenth.
- **Bare hard rock**: open chords, not barre chords, little distortion, all
  downstrokes; chords pushed onto off-beats with silence between; the rhythm
  guitar leaves room for the drums, which play the plainest beat there is.
  Timing is everything.
- **Arena rock**: a piano or keyboard figure as the opening hook; an
  arpeggiated guitar that builds between verses; a soaring voice; a solo that
  sings the tune; the chorus held back and then repeated to the end.
- **Polished eighties hard rock**: backing vocals stacked dozens deep and used
  as an instrument; two guitars as texture, playing inversions and stacked
  fourths cleanly rather than thick chords; drums sampled and programmed, huge
  and exact.
- **Boogie and heartland rock**: a chugging shuffle or boogie riff, gritty,
  simple, blues and country underneath.
- **Strummed-engine rock**: a rhythm guitar played like a drum, written on an
  acoustic and moved to electric, sixteenth strums with accents; fingerpicked
  folk guitar against it; stacked harmonies; two drummers.
- **Three guitars**: parts kept apart by register, or deliberately in the same
  register playing different things; a rhythm part that is itself a hook; a
  piano filling the gaps.
- **Synth-and-guitar midwest rock**: a lead synthesizer riff over hard rock,
  with harmonies.
- **Three singers**: each takes a verse in turn, all three in harmony on the
  chorus.
- **Indie pop**: sixties psych-pop, folk and power pop; sparse, crafted, room
  to breathe; a simple wordless hook; odd chord turns under a sweet tune.
- **Psychedelic synth pop**: a nursery-simple one-voice synth riff over an
  even simpler keyboard bass; everything distorted and compressed; drums blown
  out on purpose; orchestral strings against the noise.
- **The loop duo**: one player with a looper builds the song live, bass line
  first, then chords, then a tune, each layer entering in turn over a live
  drummer; the form is the order of entries and exits; odd meters; no vocals.
- **Rhythm and lead as one** (from blues-rock's great innovator): small triads
  on the middle strings with the bass note under, hammered and pulled
  ornaments between chord hits, so the accompaniment is always half a melody.
- **The singing instrumental**: a lead guitar as the vocalist, notes swelled
  in with no attack, bent by the bar, phrased as a singer breathes; whole
  pieces with no voice.

## Yacht Rock

Late seventies and early eighties. 80 to 110, never fast. Electric piano at
the center; clean chorused guitar; major sevenths, ninths, diminished passing
chords, ii-V-I and secondary dominants, plain triads rare; a rhythm section
that grooves without pushing, the half-time shuffle a signature; glossy
stacked backing vocals, a saxophone lead; studio players, perfect takes. One
band's two eras show the border: the strummed guitar engine first, keyboard
rhythm and blues after.

## Country Rock and Folk Rock

- Lead singer chosen first, harmonies built around the tune by ear; three-part
  harmony on choruses with guitar fills in the gaps; country lead guitar of
  double stops and pedal-steel bends; twin leads in thirds; arpeggiated
  twelve-string figures.
- Folk rock: stacked close harmonies as the hook, acoustic guitars in altered
  tunings, a ragged electric lead against them. A whole song can sit on two
  chords with a guitar harmonic ringing like a bell on the beat, a soft snare,
  and nothing else.

## The Roots Ensemble That Plays Every Style

A five-piece that is a rock band by line-up and a songbook of American styles
by repertoire: blues, gospel, country, New Orleans rhythm and blues, rock and
roll, folk ballads and waltzes, each played in its own idiom with one band's
accent. (practice)

- **Two keyboards at once**: a piano that plays rhythm (barrelhouse, gospel,
  rolling eighths) and an organ that plays color and lines: swells, circus and
  church figures, a counter-melody, never the same part as the piano. The
  organist also takes accordion and saxophone, so the color changes from song
  to song.
- **Three lead singers** who trade verses and stack rough three-part harmony,
  the parts crossing; the drummer sings lead while playing, so the drumming is
  plain, behind the beat and deep, with few fills.
- **Everyone doubles**: the drummer on mandolin, the bassist on fiddle, the
  pianist on drums; a song's line-up is chosen for it.
- **The rhythm section plays soul**: bass lines that slide and leave holes, a
  backbeat that drags, the guitar playing short stinging fills and almost no
  long solos.
- **Horns as arranged color** on some songs only: a small section written like
  a New Orleans or brass-band part, with tuba or low reeds, answering the
  voices.
- **With guests** the band becomes each guest's backing group: a Chicago blues
  band for a blues singer, a second-line piano band for a New Orleans pianist,
  a country band with fiddle for a country singer, a gospel group's support
  for a family of singers. The lesson: one set of players, many styles, by
  changing who leads, the feel and two instruments.

## Glam Rock, Hair Metal and Grunge, Compared

Three styles a decade apart that share power chords and differ in almost
everything else. Measures are from the chord dataset. (measured, practice)

- **Glam rock** (early seventies): fifties rock and roll made huge and simple.
  A stomping beat at a walking tempo, the kick and snare doubled by handclaps
  and foot stomps, often a shuffle or a floor-tom "tribal" pattern; boogie
  riffs on one or two chords; chanted choruses for a crowd; saxophone or piano
  from rock and roll; a wing that is art rock, with richer chords and odd
  forms. Major keys (minor one in five), I IV and I V, bVII in four songs in
  ten, the major II in half. A solo in one song in seven only; an outro in
  half, usually the chorus chanted to a fade. Nearer the roots and pop
  families than hard rock.
- **Hair metal** (the polished hard rock of the eighties): the most uniform
  shape in rock: intro riff, verse, pre-chorus, chorus, verse, pre-chorus,
  chorus, solo (half of all songs, the most of any style), sometimes a bridge,
  double chorus. Intro in nearly nine songs in ten. Riffs on a pedal tone: the
  open low string palm-muted in eighths with two-note power chords stabbing
  above it. Minor in over a third; bVII in half the major songs, bIII in a
  third, the major II in over half; i bVI bVII and i bVI bIII bVII in
  choruses; no sevenths. Choruses in major with stacked harmony or the whole
  band shouting the title. Solos are singable first and fast second: a tune,
  then tapping, dive bombs and a climb to a held bent note, eight or sixteen
  bars over the verse's or a new progression. Guitars doubled and panned wide;
  a very large snare with a long gated echo; kick on 1 and 3 with pushes;
  fills round the toms. Tempos 110 to 160 for anthems, 70 to 100 for the
  **power ballad**: clean arpeggios or piano and voice, the drums entering at
  the second verse or first chorus, distorted guitars at the chorus, a solo,
  and a last chorus with everything, sometimes a key up.
- **Grunge** (around 1990): punk's bluntness at hard rock's weight. Quiet
  verse, loud chorus: a clean or thin guitar, sometimes with a watery chorus
  effect, bass and voice alone, then full distortion with the same or
  neighboring chords. Power chords moved in parallel with no regard for key,
  so the major II, III and VI appear (II in half of songs, bIII in three in
  ten, vi in under a third, the least of any modern rock style); chromatic and
  semitone moves; the lowest string tuned down for one-finger chords and a
  darker sound; suspended chords and droning open strings. Minor in a third.
  Solos in a fifth of songs, short, often just the vocal tune played with
  noise. Drums heavy and plain, a big roomy snare, crashes ridden through
  choruses, a hard fill into each loud section. Feedback as an instrument. One
  wing leans to seventies rock: two guitars, one strumming funky
  sixteenth-note chords and one playing pentatonic blues leads, wider harmony
  and anthem choruses, a busier bass.
- The later radio rock that followed grunge keeps the quiet and loud but moves
  to the sixth-degree family: minor in nearly half, vi in half of major songs,
  a bridge in six in ten, and the fixed verse, chorus, verse, chorus, bridge,
  chorus shape.

## Nu Metal and Shoegaze

Two styles that are textures first and chord progressions second. Measures are
from the chord dataset. (measured, practice)

- **Nu metal** (late nineties): riff and rhythm, no display. Guitars tuned
  very low, often with a seventh string, playing syncopated one- or two-note
  riffs with rests in them, locked to the kick, borrowed from groove metal and
  from hip hop's drum patterns. Drums play a hip hop or funk backbeat, heavy
  and dry, with ghost notes; the bass is funk-influenced, sometimes slapped or
  five-string. A turntable or sampler adds scratches, loops and electronic
  beds. Verses are rapped or half-spoken over a sparse riff, a clean guitar
  figure or a loop; the chorus is sung, loud and melodic, with full guitars.
  Minor in over half the songs (two in three in the rap wing): i bVI, i bVI
  bIII bVII, i bVII, the flat sixth everywhere. Pop's shape exactly: verse,
  chorus, verse, chorus, bridge, chorus; a bridge in over half the songs,
  often a breakdown to whispers or electronics and a build back; a solo in one
  song in six or fewer. The polished wing is nearer electronic pop: keyboards,
  programmed beats under the drums, two singers, one rapping and one singing.
- **Shoegaze** (around 1990): pop songs under a wall of guitar. Simple chords,
  mostly major (minor in a quarter to three in ten): I IV and I V lead more
  strongly than in any other modern style, with I vi; major sevenths, added
  and suspended notes from open strings ringing through chord changes
  (sevenths and added notes on an eighth of chords, the most of any guitar
  style after yacht rock). Guitars strummed constantly in eighths or
  sixteenths with heavy distortion and reverb, layered until no single part is
  heard; whole chords bent slightly flat and back with the vibrato arm while
  strumming; reverb that swells before the note; volume swells; feedback held
  as a pad. Voices soft, breathy, mixed low, a tune of few long notes, often
  two voices in unison or thirds. Drums plain and steady at 90 to 130,
  sometimes a dance beat; bass on roots in eighths, the one clear line. A
  chorus in three songs in four only: many songs are a verse and a wordless
  instrumental refrain. Instrumental sections in a quarter of songs, a solo in
  one in ten, and an outro in over half, the most of any rock style: the song
  dissolves into noise. Its dreamier neighbor has cleaner guitars with chorus
  and delay and keyboards in the wall's place.
