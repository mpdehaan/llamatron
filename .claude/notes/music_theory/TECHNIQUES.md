# Techniques Read About and Not Yet Used

Each is a candidate for the song wizard, noted so that it is not lost. None is
a promise.

- A **pre-chorus** as a kind of section: a ramp of two or four bars with
  quickening chords and a drum build.
- A **stop**: the whole band silent for a beat or a bar before a chorus, or
  the tune alone over silence.
- A **key change** up a step or a half step for the last chorus.
- A **dropped or added bar** before a chorus to arrive early or make the
  listener wait.
- **Half time and double time** sections: the same tempo felt at half or twice
  the speed, for a bridge or a last chorus.
- A **breakdown**: drums and voice alone, or everything but the drums out,
  before the last chorus.
- **Pedal point**: the bass holding one note while the chords change above.
- **Harmonized hook**: a second line a third or a sixth from the hook.
- **Trading bars** in a solo, and the band dropping down as a solo begins.
- **Pickups**: a tune or a fill that begins before its section's first bar.
- **Counter-melody**: a slower second tune under the chorus, often on strings,
  keys or a backing voice.
- **Hi-hat openings, rim clicks and ride bell** as section colors for the
  drummer.
- **Dynamics inside a phrase**: a swell toward the phrase's high point and
  away.
- **Layered percussion**: tambourine or shaker entering at the chorus to lift
  it without changing the drum part.

## Playing From a Lead Sheet

A lead sheet gives a tune, chord symbols and a road map, and nothing else: the
band invents the rest by style. What players are taught to add is what an
arranger of generated songs must add. (practice)

- **Read the form first**: repeats, first and second endings, the coda. Then
  decide an intro (the last four bars, or a vamp on the first two chords) and
  an ending (a tag of the last line three times, a ritard, or a vamp and
  fade).
- **Bass by style**: pop and country, root on beat 1 and fifth on beat 3, with
  an eighth-note pickup into each new chord; rock, roots in eighths; ballad,
  roots in half notes; swing, a walking line of chord tones with a chromatic
  approach into each new chord; Latin, root and fifth in the anticipated
  rhythm. A slash chord is the only bass note the sheet dictates.
- **Chords by style**: pop, block chords or broken chords in the middle
  register in a repeating rhythm; rock, fifths and octaves low, full chords in
  eighths; jazz, shells (root and seventh, or third and seventh) and rootless
  voicings with ninths and thirteenths, played in short syncopated stabs that
  answer the tune; ballad, a stride left hand or arpeggios; gospel, full
  chords with walk-ups between them.
- **Leave space and fill it**: comp under the tune's long notes and rests,
  never on top of its busy bars; a fill in the last bar of each phrase,
  pointing at the next chord.
- **Improve the sheet**: add sevenths where the style wants them; put a ii
  before a V; approach a chord from a semitone above (tritone substitution) or
  by its own dominant; walk the bass between chords a fourth apart; substitute
  vi or iii for I to keep a long home chord moving; add a passing diminished
  chord between neighbors a tone apart.
- **Vary by chorus**: first time plain, second time fuller or higher, with a
  counter-line; a different texture for the bridge; hold back for the last
  verse and open up for the last chorus.

## Rhythm Guitar Strokes

- **The palm-muted low string** is the engine of hard rock and metal rhythm:
  short, a little soft, on the lowest string, with full chords or accents
  breaking in. A chug is mostly these with a chord on the backbeats; a gallop
  is one long and two short of them to a beat; a thrash bar is sixteenths of
  them with chords stabbed on a few. Scratching every string where the low
  string should chug sounds like funk, not metal. (practice)
- **The stab**: every string, full loudness, cut off halfway, with silence
  after. On beats 2 and 4 it is the soul and reggae backbeat chord; on every
  off-beat it is the ska and reggae skank. The silence is as much the part as
  the chord. (practice)
- **Muted strokes between strums** keep time like a clock in acoustic and funk
  playing; in disco the bar is mostly muted sixteenths with two or three
  chords let through. (practice)
- **The squeal**: one very high, short, loud note ending a low riff's phrase,
  a mark of groove metal and later hard rock. (practice)
- **Approach from a fret away**: a chord struck a half step below or above for
  an instant before it lands, the slide of grunge and groove riffs. Used on
  every chord it tires; used on changes it gives weight. (practice)
- **Chord colors as a player's habit**: leaving the third out (the power chord
  and its open sound), a suspended second or fourth for chime and ambiguity,
  an added ninth for shimmer. One color kept through a part characterizes it
  more than any single chord choice. (practice)
- **Single lines thickened**: in octaves for weight; with the scale's third
  above for the twin-lead sound; with the fourth below for the early rock and
  roll double stop. (practice)
- **A slow picked chord**, one string at a time from the bass, opens ballads;
  **four heavy down strokes** a bar is the stomp of glam and arena choruses;
  **three hits and two** on one chord is the oldest rock and roll hand-drum
  beat. (practice)

## Stretching a Song Out

- A jam is built in stages, not one long solo: the band settles on a vamp from
  the song, climbs in steps (each a little busier, higher or louder), peaks,
  and either falls back to settle and climbs again or returns to the song's
  theme. The return of the theme is the payoff, so it must be recognizable.
  (practice)
- The groove players keep one part through a stage and change at its edge;
  only the lead changes within it. A stage is as long as it needs, but its
  material is short and repeats. The lead may pass between players at a
  stage's edge. (practice)
