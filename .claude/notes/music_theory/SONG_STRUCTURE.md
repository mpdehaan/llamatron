# Song Structure

## Sections

- A song is a few kinds of section, each returning. The **verse** carries the
  story: its words change each time, its tune stays. The **chorus** carries
  the song's main idea and its hook: words and tune both return, and it is the
  high point of energy. The **bridge** (middle eight) comes once, late, and
  exists to be different, so that the last chorus feels like coming home. A
  **pre-chorus** is a short ramp between a verse and a chorus, useful when the
  two are far apart in range or energy. A **solo** takes a verse's or a
  chorus's chords with an instrument in the singer's place. An **intro** and
  an **ending** frame the rest. (practice)
- Older forms have no chorus: the **twelve-bar blues** repeats one chorus of
  twelve bars throughout, and the **thirty-two bar form** is two like phrases,
  a contrasting middle and the first phrase again, the title in the first or
  last line. (practice)
- Sections are nearly always 4, 8, 12 or 16 bars, built from phrases of 2 or
  4. An odd length is a deliberate surprise: a bar added before a chorus to
  make the listener wait, or a bar dropped to arrive early. (practice)

## Contrast Between Sections

- A chorus stands out from its verse by several of these at once: a higher
  range, longer notes, fewer and more repeated words, a simpler tune made of
  short repeated phrases, fuller instruments, a busier or more open drum part,
  and often a move to chords the verse held back. A verse may sit low and
  wordy on few pitches so that the chorus's leap is felt. (practice)
- The title usually sits at the start or the end of the chorus, on its longest
  or highest notes. (practice)
- Energy over a whole song usually rises in steps: the second verse a little
  fuller than the first, the last chorus the fullest, with a drop somewhere,
  often the bridge or a stripped-down verse, so that the last rise has
  somewhere to come from. (practice)

## The Pre-Chorus

- **What it does**: a ramp of 2, 4 or sometimes 8 bars between verse and
  chorus that makes the chorus feel arrived at. It returns with the same tune
  each time, and often the same words, so it is a second small hook.
  (practice)
- **Chords**: it leaves the verse's loop and avoids the home chord, starting
  on IV, ii or vi, the chords changing faster than in the verse (two a bar
  where the verse had one), and ends on V or IV held, wanting home, which the
  chorus's first chord gives. Where the verse and chorus share one loop, the
  pre-chorus is the only place the harmony moves. (practice)
- **Tune**: it climbs: each phrase starts a step higher, or the phrases
  shorten, so the chorus's first note is the top. (practice)
- **Band**: the drums build (kick on every beat, the snare doubling to eighths
  then sixteenths in the last bar, or a drop to half-time and then the fill);
  the bass climbs or pedals on V; guitars open from muted to ringing; a bar of
  near silence or one held chord before the chorus is as strong as a fill.
  (practice)
- **When**: rare in the 1960s (about one chart song in fourteen), one in four
  by the 1980s, and usual in later pop, pop punk and polished hard rock. Roots
  styles, blues and country seldom have one: there the verse's last line turns
  around instead. (measured)
- The first verse may go to the chorus by way of it, or the first pass may
  hold it back so that it is new the second time.

## Shapes of Whole Songs

- **Verses in a row** (blues, rock and roll, rockabilly, folk): intro, three
  to five verses with an instrumental break after the second or third, the
  title as each verse's last line (a refrain), and a tag. No chorus; variety
  comes from the break and the band.
- **Verse and chorus alternating** (roots rock, country, soul, jam): verse,
  chorus, verse, chorus, a break over the verse's chords, chorus; a bridge in
  a quarter of songs or fewer. Country often tells two verses before the first
  chorus.
- **Thirty-two bars with a middle** (early beat groups, standards, doo-wop):
  two verses ending on the title, a contrasting middle eight starting away
  from home, a last verse; then the middle and last verse again, sometimes
  with half a verse as a solo.
- **Verse, chorus, verse, chorus, bridge, chorus** (later rock, pop punk, new
  wave, radio rock): the commonest shape from the 1980s on; the bridge drops
  down or changes key color, and the last chorus comes twice, the first time
  stripped or the second time with a lift.
- **The same with a solo** (hard rock, arena rock): the solo over the verse's
  or chorus's chords after the second chorus, before or in place of the
  bridge, then a double chorus.
- **Chorus first** (soul, gospel, reggae, some pop): the hook opens the song,
  then the verse explains it.
- **Groove forms** (funk, dance): one vamp throughout, sections made by who
  plays, with one bridge that changes the chord.
- A song grows longer with the decades: 8 sections in the 1960s, 9 in the
  1970s, 11 in the 1980s, and the first chorus arrives later (bar 16, 20, 24).
  The second verse is often half the first's length in later styles, to reach
  the chorus sooner.

## How Songs Start

- An intro sets tempo, key and mood before the singer. Its length has fallen
  over time: hits of the mid-1980s averaged over twenty seconds of intro, and
  hits thirty years later about five. (measured) So eight bars suits an older
  style and two a modern one.
- The common ways: a **drum fill** of a beat to a bar into the whole band; the
  **drums alone** on the groove for one to four bars; a **count** on the
  sticks; **one instrument's riff** alone, then the band; **layers**, players
  joining one at a time at an even spacing; the **full band** on the first
  beat; and **straight in** on the verse or the chorus. (practice)
- A band lead-in tends to have stages: a setup by one thing alone, then the
  band landing, often with a crash and the hook on a lead instrument over the
  verse's chords, then the verse, where the singer's entrance is the event.
  (practice)
- Order of entrance: in groove music (funk, disco, soul, dance) drums, then
  bass, then chords, then a lead line, then the voice; in riff music (hard and
  arena rock, new wave) the riff first, then a fill, then drums and bass
  together. Bass and drums enter together or one stage apart, and the voice
  last. (practice)
- Dance music adds a part every eight bars and keeps sixteen or more bars of
  drums at the top, so that one record can be mixed into the next. (practice)
- A start with nothing to set it up, a chord instrument sounding on the first
  instant, is the least natural on a recording unless the style is meant to
  feel abrupt. (judgment)

- **Lead-ins by style**: rock and roll and rockabilly, a two- or four-bar riff
  or a pickup into the verse, sometimes the voice alone for a beat; beat
  groups, a four-bar riff or the chorus's last line played; folk rock, the
  chiming figure alone, then the band; hard rock, the riff alone twice, a
  fill, the riff with the band twice (8 bars, often 16 in the 1980s); punk and
  pop punk, a count and straight in, or four bars of guitar alone then all;
  country, a kickoff by fiddle, steel or guitar over the last line's chords
  (the turnaround), 2 to 4 bars; bluegrass, a kickoff of a full verse by one
  instrument; blues, the last four bars of the form as an intro, starting on
  V; soul, a horn line or a drum pickup into the groove; funk, the drums or
  the guitar's figure alone, then one part at a time; yacht rock, the
  keyboard's chords with a short lead line; ballads, piano or guitar alone.
- **The join into each section** matters as much as the top: a fill or a
  pickup into every chorus, a crash on its first beat, a stop of a beat or a
  bar before it; a drop to fewer players at each verse so the next chorus has
  room to rise. (practice)

## How Songs End

- The **fade** over a repeating chorus ruled recordings from the 1950s into
  the 1980s and has nearly vanished from hits since, giving way to endings
  that stop. (measured)
- Endings a band can play: a **held chord** with a crash; a **hard stop** on
  the beat after the last bar; a **big finish**, the chord held while the
  drums roll and then one last hit together; a **tag**, the chorus's last line
  two or three times; a **slowing** of the last bars into a held chord;
  **stripping down**, players leaving one at a time; the **opening riff
  alone** once more; the blues **turnaround** walked down to a held chord.
  (practice)
- **Play-outs by style**: rock and roll and blues, the turnaround and a held
  chord, or the last line tagged three times; beat groups, a tag and a held
  chord with harmony, or a short fade; sixties to eighties pop, soul and yacht
  rock, a fade over the chorus or a vamp with the singer ad-libbing, its outro
  growing from 8 bars to 16; hard and arena rock, the big finish, or a fade
  over the solo guitar; southern and jam styles, a long instrumental play-out
  that doubles the tempo or stacks solos; punk and pop punk, a hard stop, or
  the last chord left ringing; country, the last line tagged and a ritard into
  a held chord; bluegrass, a tag and the standard closing lick; funk, the vamp
  to a fade or a stop on a hit together; dance, layers out.
- **The vamp out**: two or four bars of the chorus's or a new loop repeated,
  the band adding or the singer answering, is the usual body of a fade and of
  a long play-out. A fade needs something still changing in it, or it sounds
  like waiting. (practice)
- An ending mirrors a start: layers in, layers out; riff first, riff last. An
  ending that stops needs its tension resolved before the last note, or the
  silence sounds like a cut. (practice)

## Measured: Chart Songs by Decade

From the chord and section transcriptions of about 740 chart singles, 1958 to
1991, kept on this computer only; all genres of the chart together, so a
tendency of popular song, not of one style. (measured)

- **Intros**: nine songs in ten have one. Median 4 bars in the 1960s, 5 in the
  1970s, 8 in the 1980s. The usual lengths are 4 and 8, then 2; 16-bar intros
  appear in the 1970s and are common in the 1980s. No intro at all: about one
  song in ten.
- **Verse before chorus** in more than eight songs in ten; a chorus first in
  about 19% (1960s) falling to 8% (1980s).
- **Bars before the first chorus**: median 16 (1960s), 20 (1970s), 24 (1980s).
  Songs take longer to arrive as decades pass.
- **Lengths**: a verse's median is 12 bars in every decade (8, 12 and 16 all
  common); a chorus 8; a bridge 8; a solo 8 to 9.
- **What a song has**: a chorus in 70% (1960s), 85% (1970s), 94% (1980s); a
  bridge in 30 to 45%; an instrumental solo in 28% (1960s), 35% (1970s), 45%
  (1980s); a pre-chorus in 7%, 12%, 23%; an interlude in 21%, 28%, 37%.
- **Endings**: a fade in more than half of all songs, 56% (1960s) to 69%
  (1980s). An outro section in about four in ten, its median 8 bars (1960s),
  12 (1970s), 16 (1980s). Ending cold on a chorus or verse is the minority,
  commonest early.
- **Size**: median 80 bars and 8 sections (1960s), 94 and 9 (1970s), 112 and
  11 (1980s).

## How Intros Begin and Exit, at Length

(practice throughout, but for the one figure marked measured)

### How Intros Begin

Rock and pop intros usually run four or eight bars and come in a handful of
standard types:

- **The riff or hook alone.** One instrument states the song's signature idea,
  and the others enter in layers: guitar, then hi-hat, then full drums and
  bass. "Smoke on the Water" is the textbook case.
- **The chorus without the vocal.** The band plays the chorus chords, often
  with the hook on an instrument, so the chorus feels familiar when it
  arrives.
- **A verse vamp.** Two or four bars of the verse groove, waiting for the
  singer.
- **A drum intro.** A count-in, a pickup fill or a signature beat.
- **An atmospheric build.** A pad, noise or a filtered version of the track
  that swells into the song.
- **A cold open.** The vocal starts immediately, sometimes with the chorus
  itself.

Intros have shrunk a lot in the streaming era. A study by Hubert Léveillé
Gauvin found that the average intro of hit songs fell from over 20 seconds in
the mid-1980s to about 5 by 2015, because listeners can skip. (measured)

### How Intros Exit

The handoff into the first verse is usually one of these:

- **Fill and crash.** A drum fill sets up the verse downbeat.
- **The drop-down.** The band thins out as the vocal enters, often to just
  bass and drums. The intro promises the energy of the chorus and the verse
  pulls back from it.
- **A stop.** The band hits a chord and leaves a beat of silence, and the
  singer's pickup notes lead into the verse over the gap.
- **A build.** A crescendo or a held dominant chord resolves on the verse's
  first beat.
- **No exit at all.** In riff-based songs, the riff carries on under the
  verse.

Intro material tends to return later. It often comes back as a short
turnaround after the first chorus, and again as the outro to bookend the song.

The exits from the song itself mirror these. The standard ones are the fade,
the hard stop on a final hit, the tag (the last line repeated three times),
the ritardando, and the last chord left to ring.

## Section Lengths, Bridges and Transitions, at Length

throughout)

### Typical Lengths

Nearly everything in rock and pop comes in multiples of four bars, and most
sections are 8 or 16. These are typical lengths in 4/4:

| Section | Typical length | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intro | 4 or 8 bars | Often 2 or none in current pop |
| Verse | 8 or 16 | 12 in blues-based songs |
| Pre-chorus | 4 or 8 | |
| Chorus | 8 or 16 | Often an 8-bar idea played twice |
| Post-chorus | 4 or 8 | An instrumental or chanted hook |
| Turnaround | 2 or 4 | A short reset between chorus and next verse |
| Bridge | 8 | Hence the British name "middle eight"; sometimes 4 or 16 |
| Solo | 8 or 16 | 12 over a blues form |
| Breakdown | 4 or 8 | |
| Outro | 4 or 8, or a fade | |

### Solos

A solo usually borrows a section's chords, so it inherits that section's
length. Most are played over the verse or chorus progression. Three sizes
cover most cases:

- 8 bars: a half-verse solo, common in pop and radio edits.
- 16 bars: the standard rock solo, one full verse or chorus.
- 12 or 24 bars: one or two choruses of a blues.

Classic rock stretches well past this, especially at the end of a song.
Closing solos like the ones in "Comfortably Numb" and "Free Bird" run for
dozens of bars, and live versions go longer. Current pop tends the other way:
often there is no solo, or the slot goes to a 4- or 8-bar instrumental hook.

### Thinking in Seconds

Tempo changes what a bar count means, so it helps to convert. At 120 BPM there
are 30 bars per minute, so 8 bars is 16 seconds and a 3:30 song is about 105
bars. Most sections last 15 to 30 seconds whatever the tempo, which is why
fast songs use 16-bar sections where slow ballads use 8.

### Exceptions

Writers often bend the grid on purpose:

- **Extensions**: an extra bar or two at the end of a section to delay the
  chorus.
- **Cut bars**: a bar of 2/4, or a dropped bar, so the next section arrives
  early.
- **Odd phrases**: the verse of "Yesterday" is seven bars long.
- **Short second verses**: the second verse is often half the length of the
  first, to reach the chorus sooner.

These deviations work for the same reason as other broken rules
(`PLAYER_STYLE.md`): the four-bar habit is strong enough that a listener feels
the extra or missing bar.

### Bridges

A bridge is designed in opposition to the rest of the song: whatever the verse
and chorus have used most, the bridge uses least. Transitions run on three
levers: a signal that change is coming, a shift in energy, and a harmonic pull
from an unstable chord to a stable one.

The bridge usually comes once, after the second chorus, and its job is to make
the final chorus sound fresh. Writers look at what the song has done so far
and move away from it:

- **Harmony.** If the verse and chorus start on the tonic, the bridge starts
  somewhere else, most often on IV, vi or ii. A major-key song often leans
  toward its relative minor here. Borrowed chords like flat VI and flat VII,
  or a brief change of key, are common.
- **Harmonic rhythm.** A song built on a fast four-chord loop might hold one
  chord for the whole bridge. A song with slow, static harmony might start
  changing chords twice a bar.
- **Melody.** The bridge takes a register the chorus has not used, and changes
  the phrasing. If earlier melodies start on the downbeat, the bridge starts
  off the beat. Short busy phrases give way to long notes, or the reverse.
- **Groove.** Half-time is the classic move. Others are double-time, stop-time
  hits, or dropping the drums entirely.
- **Texture.** A loud song strips down to voice and one instrument. A sparse
  song may add strings or stacked vocals.
- **Lyrics.** The bridge gives a new angle: a twist, a different point in
  time, or the wider view.

The type of song shapes the choice: loop-based pop often keeps the same chords
and changes only the production (the "breakdown bridge"); rock often uses a
new riff, a new key, or the guitar solo as the bridge; EDM uses the breakdown
before the final drop; hip-hop may switch the beat.

### Verse Into Chorus: The Lift

- The pre-chorus builds. The melody rises, the chords change faster, and the
  section ends on an unstable chord, usually V or IV, that the chorus
  resolves.
- Drums signal it. The usual signals are a snare build, a fill and a crash on
  the chorus downbeat.
- The drop-out. The band cuts out for the last beat or bar, leaving silence or
  the vocal alone, and the chorus then enters at full strength. Taking energy
  away just before adding it is one of the most reliable tricks in arranging.
- The vocal pickup. The first words of the chorus begin before the bar line.
- The arrangement widens. The melody jumps up in register, and backing vocals,
  doubled guitars and open hi-hats come in.

### Chorus Into Verse: The Come-Down

- A turnaround. Two or four bars of the intro riff reset the song.
- A hard drop. The band crashes on the verse downbeat and thins out at once.
- A ring-over. The last chorus chord sustains into the verse's first bar.
- A partial retreat. The second verse keeps something from the chorus, such as
  extra percussion or a counter-line, so the energy does not fall all the way
  back to verse one.

### Into the Bridge

- A direct cut. The chorus ends and the bridge starts on a new chord with a
  new texture. The abruptness is deliberate.
- A pivot. The chorus's final tonic chord is reinterpreted, for example as V
  of IV, leading smoothly into a bridge that starts on IV. Or a deceptive move
  to vi opens the bridge in the relative minor.
- A drop-out. Everything cuts but a pad or one instrument.
- A fill into half-time.

### Out of the Bridge

This is usually the biggest transition in the song.

- Dominant preparation. The bridge ends on V, often held for a bar or two with
  a crescendo, a snare roll and a rising line. Classical theory calls this the
  retransition.
- Layered rebuild. After a breakdown, instruments re-enter one at a time,
  ending with the song's largest fill.
- Stop and slam. A beat of silence, then the full chorus.
- The breakdown chorus. The bridge leads into a quiet chorus with voice and
  minimal backing, and then the full band enters for the repeat.
- The key change. The bridge ends on V of a key a half step or whole step
  higher, and the last chorus arrives in the new key.

### Into the Ending

- The final chorus is doubled, with ad-libs, higher harmonies or a new
  counter-melody on the repeat.
- The tag. The last line repeats two or three times. Often a deceptive cadence
  (V to vi) delays the final tonic, so the true ending feels earned.
- An outro vamp. The hook or intro riff loops, under a fade or under solos.
- A bookend. The intro returns to close the song.
- A hard ending. The band plays a final figure together and stops, or holds
  the last chord while the cymbals swell. Sometimes it slows down first.
- A cold stop on the last word.
- A coda with new material, as in "Hey Jude" and "Layla".

### Intro Into the First Verse

The exits are above ("How Intros Begin and Exit, at Length"): fill and crash,
the drop-down, the stop with a vocal pickup, and the build. Two further
points:

- **Carry-over.** Part of the intro usually continues, such as the riff, the
  drum groove or a pad, so the verse sounds like the same song.
- **Headroom.** The first verse is normally the sparsest full section.
  Starting small leaves room for every later section to grow.

## What Makes a Chorus Good

- A chorus differs from its verse on at least three counts: register, its
  centre a third to a fifth up; longer notes; fewer notes; a plainer, more
  repeated rhythm; starting on or just before the downbeat where the verse
  started after it. (practice)
- The hook is at an edge of the chorus, never inside a line: at the start of
  the first line or the end of the last, best both. A chorus that closes on
  something other than the hook gives up the place a listener remembers.
  (practice)
- The arrival is written: in the bar before, the band thins or stops, a fill,
  the harmony on the chord that pulls home, the melody rising or silent; then
  the full band on the first beat. The chorus ends with the hook on the home
  note, held, and a bar of space. It is as long as the verse or half of it,
  never longer the first time; the last may double. (practice)
- The band lifts a chorus by adding one or two things, not everything: open
  cymbals, a firmer bass, a harmony voice on the hook alone, a held pad or
  doubled guitar. How much a chorus lifts is one thing to a listener, moving
  register, note length, density and the band together, and it means something
  else in each genre: in a dance track the lift is the arrangement. (practice)
- A chorus fails when it does not lift, is too like the verse, is too busy,
  buries its hook, has no arrival or overstays; each can be measured and a
  failed one written again. (practice)

## Long Pieces in Several Parts

- A long piece that changes key, tempo, mood and material is made of parts, a
  part being a new frame, not a long song: each part has its own key, tempo,
  range, register plan, motifs, riffs and melodies. An ordinary song is a
  piece of one part. A long build with no change of frame may still be one
  part with many melodies. (practice)
- A kind of section is a role that gives defaults, not the identity of a
  melody: a part may hold several unrelated verse-like melodies, or a late
  melody that is neither verse, chorus nor bridge, so melodies are named
  things that sections point at. (practice)
- What a part is known by is its main idea, of a kind: a sung hook, which
  returns often; a refrain line, which closes each verse; an instrumental
  theme, stated, left and restated; a riff; an arrival melody, heard once,
  late, at the peak, with only its rhythm hinted before; or none, for a chain
  of fragments or a solo. (practice)
- What makes parts one work and not a medley, in order of effect: one long arc
  of energy and register over the whole piece, into which each part is scaled,
  so that the highest point is where the arc says and not where a kind of
  section says; a shared motif transformed, its rhythm kept with new pitches
  the most audible link; a recall of earlier material later, refitted to the
  key and tempo and usually thinned; a written transition between parts (a
  hard cut, a held chord or silence, a pivot chord, a tempo ramp, a link by
  drums or one instrument, a riff that changes under the listener); keys
  related simply and tempos in simple ratios. (practice)
- In a long build the melody stays and the accompaniment grows at each return,
  so how a return differs has two halves, the melody's and the
  accompaniment's. (practice)
- A listener holds few ideas: seldom more than five or six motifs in a whole
  long piece, one or two in a part. (practice)
- What rules do poorly and hands do well: a middle of short contrasting
  fragments with voices answering, a climactic solo that is remembered,
  expressive slides and bends, the seam between parts, and tempo that grows as
  if played. (practice)

## What Long Pieces Ask of a Model of a Song

- A frame is inherited and overridden, never owned at one level: the piece
  gives key, tempo, meter and band, a part may change them, and a section may
  change them again, since a verse can sit in another key than the riff and
  chorus around it. A section may also start in one key and end in another,
  and a value such as tempo or energy may ramp across a part with no cut.
  (practice)
- A new part begins where the main idea changes and at least two of key,
  tempo, meter and band change, at a seam: a cut, a hold or a link. Anything
  less is a section's own change or a ramp. (practice)
- Main ideas and motifs belong to the whole piece and a part refers to them: a
  part may have none, one or two equal ones, and a late part may only recall
  an earlier one. (practice)
- What a section holds is one of four things: a melody, a recall, a chain of
  short fragments each with its own voices, key and loudness, or a solo, free
  lead over its own or a borrowed progression. (practice)
- In long builds the layering is the form: which instrument enters or leaves
  at which section is a schedule of its own, apart from the notes, and the
  melody hardly changes. (practice)
- A riff has its own key and chords, may frame a section in another key, and
  may lie under a sung line as accompaniment. (practice)
- A transition can be a small section with material, meter and a key path of
  its own. A piece has an ending of a kind: a full stop, a fade, a thinning to
  one voice, a slowing, free time, and a final key. A bar may be longer or
  shorter than its neighbours, or change meter for one bar. A second lead may
  answer in the melody's gaps. (practice)


## Intros as Shapes (the professor's review of an intro panel)

(practice throughout)

- An arranger picks a known intro shape and then adjusts two or three things;
  nobody fills in a table of material, parts, players and bars. Bars follow
  the phrase (4 or 8, 2 for a tag) and the order players join follows the
  genre.
- A real multi-part intro is two gestures, a statement and an arrival, with
  now and then a prelude in front: three parts at most.
- The shapes: a count or fill and straight in (0 to 1 bar: punk, sixties pop,
  rock and roll, a blues shuffle); the riff alone and then the band on the
  riff (4+4, 2+2 when fast: hard rock, arena rock); the hook stated by an
  instrument and then a drop to the verse groove (4 or 8: sixties pop, soul,
  arena rock, synth pop); a vamp on one or two chords by the rhythm section
  with a lick over it (2 or 4: soul, funk, disco); a layered build, one
  element a phrase (8 to 32: house, techno, disco, funk); a prelude and then
  the hit (atmosphere or one instrument for 4 to 8, then the band lands on the
  riff or hook: arena rock, electronic); the turnaround from the top (the
  form's last 4 bars or the V chord: blues, rock and roll); chorus first (the
  song opens on the sung chorus, which is a decision of form).
- Drums alone is no shape of its own: it is the fill lengthened or the first
  layer of a build.
- Defaults: arena rock a prelude and hit or the riff alone, 8 to 16 bars; hard
  rock the riff alone then the band, 4+4; punk a count, or 2+2; folk rock one
  player's figure then the band, 4+4; sixties pop the hook stated in 4 or a
  pickup, never over 8; soul a vamp with a lick, 4 (8 for a ballad); funk a
  vamp or a short build from drums or guitar, 4 to 8; disco a build from the
  drums, 16; house and techno a build from the kick, 16 to 32; blues the
  turnaround, 4.
- The rule that matters more than the number of parts: an intro must differ
  from the first verse in at least one of density, level, register or
  material, so that the verse is heard as a change. The whole band on the
  verse's chords at the verse's level is dull at any length.
- Holding the bass back for the verse or the chorus is the cheapest intro
  device that works.
- Exits by shape: a fill and crash after the riff, the prelude or the
  turnaround, never after a fill that was the intro and never in house or
  techno; a drop to bass and drums only after an intro at the chorus's level;
  a stop with a beat of silence after the riff, the hook, the prelude or the
  turnaround, never after a vamp; a build on a held chord after a prelude or a
  layered build; carrying on under the verse is the one exit of a vamp and of
  a layered build, and the instrument that stated the hook stops for the
  singer even where the groove carries on. Electronic styles also leave by a
  riser's release or a bar without drums.
- What returns: a riff or a stated hook comes back after the chorus nearly
  always; a prelude returns only as the outro; a layered build returns
  reversed as the outro; a count and a turnaround do not return.
- The intro is decided after the hook, the riff, the chords and the form
  exist, since it quotes them, and together with the turnarounds and the
  ending, as one step of framing the song.

## What Makes a Bridge a Bridge and a Solo a Solo (the professor, as rules a program can follow)

(practice)

- A bridge is eight bars, never fewer in arena rock, where four is heard as a
  link. It begins off the home chord, usually on IV, vi or bVII, uses none of
  the chorus's progression, and ends on V, held, to launch the last chorus.
  Its melody is new, with nothing of the hook, a different rhythm and longer
  notes, under the hook's top. The band changes texture: drums to half time or
  toms, guitars thinner or sustained, then a build over the last two bars.
- A solo is eight or sixteen bars over the verse's or the chorus's chords with
  the band at full intensity, the rhythm guitar and bass keeping the groove.
  It starts low, quoting or paraphrasing the hook's rhythm, speaks in
  sentences of two bars with rests between and never as a stream, rises in
  register and in notes throughout, uses bends and held notes, peaks on its
  highest note in the last two bars and lands on a note of the chord into the
  next section.


## Intros of a Listening Demo

- A demo heard rather than mixed keeps its intro to two bars, four at most,
  under about eight seconds, with the hook in by twenty to thirty seconds. The
  long drum intros of dance records are there for a DJ to mix into, and with
  no effects moving they only wait. (practice)
- With no effects, a build is made by adding a part every four to eight bars,
  taking the kick and bass out before a peak, a snare roll doubling its speed,
  a bar of space, then everything at once; a filter opened over eight to
  sixteen bars, never reset every two. (practice)
