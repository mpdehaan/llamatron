# Harmony

- Most popular music uses three to five chords from one key. In a major key
  the chords on the first, fourth and fifth steps are major and carry most
  songs; the chord on the sixth step, minor, is the usual fourth chord; those
  on the second and third add gentler colors. (practice)
- Chords have jobs: **home** (the first step), **away** (the fourth, the
  second) and **wanting home** (the fifth). A progression is a trip among
  them, and where a section's last chord leaves the listener, at home or
  wanting it, decides how the next section arrives. (practice)
- A loop of four chords, one a bar, is the commonest engine; the same four in
  another order have another character because the starting chord and the last
  chord change. Starting a loop on the minor sixth step makes a major key
  sound sad without changing key. (practice)
- **Borrowed chords** come from the minor key of the same name: the flat
  seventh, flat sixth and flat third major chords, and the minor fourth. The
  flat seventh is the mark of much rock; the minor fourth of wistful pop.
  (practice)
- **Sections and chords**: a verse often circles home; a pre-chorus moves away
  and ends wanting home; a chorus arrives home on its first bar or withholds
  home until its title lands; a bridge commonly starts on the fourth or the
  sixth step and ends on the fifth. A chorus on the same chords as its verse
  leans on melody and instruments for contrast. (practice)
- **Rate of change**: chords usually change once a bar or once every two;
  quickening the changes near a section's end raises tension, and one held
  chord under a groove (funk, dance) throws all the weight onto rhythm.
  (practice)
- **The blues** treats all three main chords as dominant sevenths and keeps
  its twelve-bar order; the tune's lowered third against the chord's raised
  third is the sound itself, not a mistake. (practice)
- **Voicing**: which notes of a chord, in what order and how spread. Moving
  each note the smallest distance to the next chord makes parts smooth; guitar
  shapes, keyboard hands and horn sections each have habitual spreads. The
  bass usually takes the root on the first beat and walks or leaps to the next
  root. An inversion, another chord note in the bass, smooths a bass line.
  (practice)
- Rules are habits of a style. Using a progression against its genre's habit
  is a way to sound fresh, and the notes of each genre say what its habits are
  so that bending one is a choice. (judgment)

## Measured: Chord Loops in Chart Songs by Decade

Same corpus as the measures in `SONG_STRUCTURE.md`; each section reduced to
its shortest repeating run of chords. (measured)

- **Two chords are the commonest verse**: I IV leads every decade from the
  1960s on (14%, 6%, 6% of verses), with I V and, from the 1970s, I bVII
  beside it. A verse of two chords under a moving tune is normal, not lazy.
- **The 1950s and early 1960s** are I IV I V, I V and the I vi IV V loop;
  three distinct chords in a section.
- **From the 1960s** a section has a median of four distinct chords, and no
  loop has more than a seventh of the songs: variety is the rule, so shares
  should be spread, with a long tail.
- **The flat seven arrives in the 1970s** (I bVII IV, I bVII) and is the
  second commonest verse by the 1980s. Minor loops with bVI and bVII (i bVI
  bVII, i bVII) are a 1980s chorus sound.
- **I V vi IV is rare before the 1990s** in chart songs (about 2% of 1980s
  choruses): it belongs to later styles.
- **Choruses** favor I IV, I IV V and the IV V I arrival; a chorus that starts
  on IV is common.
- **Bridges start away from I**: on IV most of all (IV I IV V, IV V, IV I),
  then II V and III in the 1960s, and bVI bVII in the 1980s. A bridge that
  begins on the home chord is unusual.
- From a published corpus study of rock: IV is the commonest chord after I and
  the commonest before it; bVII goes to I, IV or bVI and seldom to ii, V or
  vi. In country about four chords in five are I, IV or V. Funk sits on one
  dominant ninth chord and changes in the bridge. Jazz is ii V I, the I VI ii
  V turnaround, the twelve-bar blues with ii V added, and 32 bars as AABA.
  Bluegrass is I IV V with a minor chord in perhaps one song in twenty:
  kickoff, verse, chorus, break over the verse, and a tag.

## Measured: Chords by Genre

From an open dataset of the chords of several hundred thousand songs, each
with genre tags, kept on this computer only. The chords came from pages people
made, so quality is uneven, the key of each song is guessed from its chords,
and a song's tags are those of its performer. Shares are of songs carrying a
tag; trust differences between genres more than any one number. (measured)

**Three families of rock.** Subgenres fall into three harmonic families, and
blending them makes mud:

- *Roots* (rock and roll, rockabilly, southern, jam, country rock, blues
  rock): major keys (minor in one song in five or fewer), I IV V in two- and
  four-chord loops, dominant sevenths on one chord in six to ten, vi in about
  a third of songs, few other minor chords. The IV I V I loop is as common as
  I IV.
- *Flat-seven* (hard rock, polished eighties hard rock, progressive, grunge,
  and classic rock between this and roots): bVII in about half of major-key
  songs, bIII in a third, the major II in half, vi in a third; minor keys in a
  third or more, as i bVII and i bVI bVII; almost no sevenths. Power chords
  blur major and minor, which is why II, III and VI appear as majors.
- *Sixth-degree* (pop punk, emo, skate punk, ska punk, later radio rock): vi
  in two songs in three to three in four, bVII in one in six or fewer, plain
  triads with no sevenths at all. Loops are I V vi IV and its rotations, I IV
  vi V, I V IV, and in minor i bVI bIII bVII. Later radio rock is minor in
  nearly half its songs.

**By genre.**

- *British beat and sixties pop*: major (minor one in six), I IV and I V, bVII
  already in a third of songs, vi in four in ten, bridges on IV with II V to
  return. Doo-wop: I vi IV V in a tenth of verses and choruses, I vi ii V
  beside it.
- *Folk rock, heartland, power pop, soft rock*: I IV and IV I, bVII in a
  third, ii and vi each in four or five in ten.
- *Yacht rock*: sevenths and extensions on one chord in five (soft rock one in
  eleven, classic rock one in fourteen), slash chords on one in twelve, the
  minor iv in a fifth of songs, ii in six in ten, five distinct chords in a
  chorus.
- *New wave and synthpop*: minor in a third to nearly half; bVII in four in
  ten; i bVII, i bVI, i bVI bVII; few sevenths.
- *Blues*: I IV I V and I IV lead; three distinct chords in a verse; dominant
  sevenths on a quarter of chords in the traditional kind, one in seven in the
  electric kind; vi in a fifth of songs only.
- *Country*: minor keys under one song in ten. IV I V I is the leading chorus
  (one in eight of the older kinds, one in six in bluegrass and western
  swing). Older country has three distinct chords a section and dominant
  sevenths on one in seven; the major II before V in four in ten (western
  swing and classic). Contemporary country moves to the sixth-degree family:
  vi in six songs in ten, I V vi IV among its choruses. Bluegrass has almost
  no borrowed chords.
- *Soul, the Detroit kind, disco, funk*: sevenths and extensions on a fifth to
  a quarter of chords; ii in half to six in ten; iii in four to five in ten;
  the minor iv in a fifth; I IV leads, then I ii and I vi ii V. Funk and disco
  are minor in a third to four in ten, with i IV (the Dorian vamp) and i v.
  Memphis soul is plainer: sevenths on one in ten.
- *Jazz and vocal standards*: eight or nine distinct chords a song, five a
  section; sevenths on a quarter, dominants on a fifth; ii in seven in ten,
  the major II, III and VI in half; I vi ii V the commonest loop. Bossa nova:
  extensions on four chords in ten, the minor iv in four songs in ten.
- *Reggae*: three chords a section, almost nothing borrowed; I ii, i v, i
  bVII, I IV; ska shares the punk family's chords.
- *Electronic dance*: minor in six in ten; i bVI bIII bVII and its rotations;
  four chords for the whole song.
- I V vi IV is absent from the leading loops of every style before punk and
  appears from the 1990s on.

## Modes in Rock and Pop

Rock is seldom plain major or minor: most of it mixes a major or minor home
chord with chords from a neighboring mode. A mode is heard from one or two
marker chords, and a schema can color a section without the whole song being
in the mode. (practice)

- **Mixolydian** (major with a flat seventh): marker bVII, and the V chord
  avoided or minor. Loops: I bVII; I bVII IV; bVII IV I as a cadence in place
  of V I. The sound of sixties psychedelia, southern and jam styles, and
  seventies hard rock. The tune uses the flat seventh as a resting note and
  avoids the leading tone.
- **Dorian** (minor with a raised sixth): marker a major IV under a minor home
  chord. Loops: i IV (the shuttle); i bIII IV; i IV bVII. Funk, Latin rock,
  modal jam vamps, organ-led rock. The tune leans on the raised sixth; it is
  the brightest minor.
- **Aeolian** (natural minor): markers bVI and bVII with a minor v or none.
  Loops: i bVII bVI bVII; the cadence bVI bVII i; i bVI bIII bVII. Hard rock,
  metal, eighties and later pop. A major V borrowed into it (harmonic minor)
  is an older, more dramatic color.
- **Phrygian** (minor with a flat second): marker bII over a minor home chord.
  Loops: i bII; i bII bIII. Metal, surf, flamenco color. Rare (two or three
  songs in a hundred) and unmistakable.
- **Lydian** (major with a raised fourth): marker a major II over the home
  chord with no IV. Loops: I II; I II IV I. Dreamy and unresolved; film
  scores, progressive rock, shoegaze. Theorists argue over whether it is a
  home at all.
- **Blues mixture**: major chords I IV V with a tune on the minor pentatonic:
  flat third and flat seventh sung against major chords. The commonest "mode"
  of roots rock, and why bIII and bVII appear as chords in major keys.
- **Double-tonic**: a loop that could be heard in two keys (vi IV I V as minor
  or major). Later pop lives here; the tune decides.
- Measured in the chord dataset by decade (rock, pop, soul and their kin):
  songs starting on a minor chord grow from 6% (1950s) to 13%, 19%, 25% and
  about 30% from the 2000s. bVII is in 14% of major songs in the 1950s, 29% to
  32% from the 1960s to the 1980s, then falls to 12%. bVII with no V at all
  (Mixolydian proper) is 8 to 9% from the 1960s to the 1990s. A minor song
  with a major IV and no minor iv (Dorian) is about a quarter of minor songs
  in every decade. The major II falls from a third of songs in the 1950s to a
  fifth; the minor iv from a tenth to a twentieth. A flat second in minor
  stays near one song in ten minor songs. (measured)
- **What a mode changes for a band**: the bass's passing notes, the scale of
  every fill and solo, which chord tones the keys add, and where the tune
  rests. A solo in the wrong mode over a modal loop is the commonest wrong
  note in rock.

## The Adventurous Pop Writer of the Mid-Sixties

One strand of sixties pop took harmony and arrangement much further than its
neighbors; its habits make pop that is not boring. Not typical of the era, and
to be used as a color with its own share. (practice)

- **The bass is seldom on the root.** Chords stand on their third or fifth;
  the home chord most of all appears with its fifth in the bass, so that home
  never feels settled until the chorus or the last bar. The bass moves by step
  or chromatically and is a tune.
- **Bass and chords disagree**: the bass line implies one progression while
  the chords above imply another; a pedal bass under moving chords, or a
  falling bass under a held chord.
- **Leaning to the subdominant side**: IV, ii and the minor iv more than V;
  phrases turn around the most charged chord in the middle and not around a
  final cadence. Plain V to I is saved up.
- **The key is in doubt**: a verse can sit a third away from where the chorus
  lands; sections change key by a third (to VI, bVI or bIII) or step up by a
  semitone or tone into the chorus; a brief slide into a foreign key and
  straight back.
- **Chord colors**: sixths, major sevenths, ninths, suspended and diminished
  chords as passing chords, half-diminished chords at the tensest point; major
  swapped for minor on the same root.
- **A chorus that climbs**: the same phrase repeated a whole step higher each
  time, three times, then falling back to the verse's key.
- **A minor verse falling by step** (i bVII bVI V) answered by a chorus in the
  relative major.
- **Episodes**: a song assembled from contrasting sections that do not return
  in order: verse and chorus twice, then a new slow section on held organ
  chords with a shaker on every beat, then a fragment of the chorus stepping
  down, then a short contrapuntal close. Six sections in three and a half
  minutes; joins hidden by reverb or made abruptly on purpose.
- **Orchestration as hook**: two unlike instruments doubling one line;
  keyboards doubled slightly out of tune; bass doubled by two basses, played
  with a pick, sometimes high as a lead voice; unusual colors (harpsichord,
  tack piano, French horn, accordion, bass harmonica, sleigh bells, timpani,
  bicycle bell, an electronic whistle of gliding pitch); percussion as color
  and not as a backbeat; instruments held back and entered one at a time;
  stacked wordless vocal harmony in close voicing with counterpoint.
- **Forms**: thirty-two bar and verse-chorus both; tempo changes inside a
  song; phrases of odd length.

## Naming intervals and sixes (the professor's review)

- Players say "six" for the chord and "sixth" for the interval: C E G A is a six chord, written C6; C E♭ G A a minor six,
  written Cm6 or C-6. A minor six chord holds a major 6th, so a "minor sixth" interval beside a "minor sixth chord" misleads.
- Lead sheets never spell two notes as a chord: a doubled line is written in words, "in 3rds", "in 6ths", "octaves". "8va"
  means play the line an octave up, not doubled, and is kept away from doubling.
- Interval symbols carry their quality letter (P, m, M): a Live user reads "+8" as eight half steps, as Ableton's Chord
  effect writes intervals, and a jazz player reads "+4" as a raised fourth, "+" meaning sharp or augmented in older charts.
- Chord names follow speech, with spoken numbers ("major seven", "dim seven"); intervals take written ordinals ("minor 3rd").
  Jazz writes minor as "-" and major seven as a triangle, and a bare "Csus" often means C7sus4; pop writes "m" and "maj7".

## Chord charts for a learner (the professor's review)

- A chart lists the harmony as a teacher writes it on the board, not every sound: one chord a bar in 2/4 and 3/4, half a
  bar in 4/4, a beat-long change kept only where the music truly changes, at a cadence or a passing chord.
- A stride, a bass on the beat and a chord off it, is one chord played in two halves, never a run of slash chords. A slash
  earns its place where a held bass walks by step, and in the cadential six-four before the dominant.
- The harmony is read from the accompaniment; the melody may at most confirm a missing third. A melody's notes counted as
  harmony give labels such as major elevenths that the composer never wrote.
- A chord named by what it does teaches more than its spelling: a major or dominant chord a fifth above a chord of the key
  is that chord's secondary dominant (V7/V), and only a chord from the parallel minor is borrowed (iv).
- At the sevenths level a seventh is named outright, one card per chord name; an inversion on a chord card is fingering, not
  harmony.
- The form is what a learner memorises: strains labelled, each printed once with its repeat, the key marked where it
  changes. The page of chord changes, which keys stay and which move, with how often the song makes each move, is the best
  teaching page a packet has.
