# Drum Machines and Electronic Percussion

Machines are named here, as products that shaped styles; no artist or record
is. What a machine could and could not do is why whole genres sound as they
do, so a machine's limits are a style's rules.

## What the Step Sequencer Did to Rhythm

- A bar is sixteen steps, each on or off for each sound, with an **accent**
  that makes chosen steps louder. Patterns of one bar, chained into songs,
  with an A and a B variation and a fill for each, are how machine music is
  thought: loop, variation, fill. (practice)
- Everything lands exactly on the grid unless **swing** (shuffle) is on: every
  second sixteenth played late. Swing is given as the share of a pair of
  sixteenths the first one takes: 50 percent is straight, about 66 is a full
  triplet shuffle, and the values between, 54 to 62, are the "feel" settings,
  a lean too small to hear as a shuffle and large enough to move a body.
  (practice)
- A machine has two or three loudnesses where a drummer has dozens, so machine
  grooves get their life from pattern, accent, swing, sound choice and tuning,
  and from muting and unmuting parts. (practice)

## The Machines and What Each Gave

- **Preset rhythm boxes of the 1970s** (the CR-78 and its kin): soft analog
  ticks and thumps, preset rock, waltz, bossa and shuffle patterns with a few
  variations; the sound of early new wave and synth-pop, used as a texture as
  much as a drummer.
- **The 1980 analog drum machine**: all analog. A long booming kick that can be tuned and held like
  a bass note, a snappy thin snare, a clap, a cowbell, ticking hats.
  Sixteen-step programming with accent. The base of electro, early hip hop,
  Miami bass and, later, trap, where the kick is the bass line and the hats
  roll in fast subdivisions.
- **The 1983 analog drum machine**: analog drums with sampled cymbals, a hard punching kick, a
  per-pattern shuffle, and MIDI. The kick on every beat with an open hat
  between is house; the same machine driven harder is techno.
- **The first sampled drum machine** and its forerunner: samples of real drums, tunable, with swing
  and with quantizing of what is played in on pads. The drum sound of 1980s
  pop and rock: big, clean, exact, a huge snare (often with a cut-off reverb)
  on two and four, hand claps and tambourine layered on, sixteenth-note hats
  played evenly, tom fills that run down the kit.
- **DMX**: sampled drums with a punchy, dry, hard sound; early hip hop, dance
  pop and new wave, heavy kick-and-snare patterns.
- **The early sampling drum machines** onward: samplers with pads. Drums taken from
  records, low-resolution and gritty, played in by hand and swung; chopped
  breaks. The sampling drum machine's pads and its swing made the laid-back, late-snare feel of
  boom bap; its sixteen pads made playing a pattern like an instrument normal.
- **HR-16**, **SR-16**, **SR-18**: affordable boxes of realistic sampled kits
  with preset patterns played in by drummers, each with an A and a B part and
  a fill for each, a loudness-sensitive sound that changes tone with force,
  and a footswitch to move through a song. The songwriter's and the demo's
  drummer from the late 1980s on: rock, pop and country backing without a kit.
- **The drum-synth groovebox**: sixteen tracks, each a small synthesizer chosen from
  families: models of the classic analog machines, FM percussion, sampled
  acoustic drums, and physical models of struck things. Its sequencer lets any
  step carry its own value for any setting (a parameter lock), so one track
  can be a different sound on every step; each track has an LFO; kits are kept
  apart from patterns; tracks are muted and brought back as a performance. The
  instrument of much IDM and minimal techno. Later machines of the family
  added steps that play only on some passes or by chance.

## Details Worth Having

- **A, B and fill** on the session boxes: asking for a fill while A plays
  gives A's fill from that point of the bar to its end and then B; asking
  during B gives B's fill and then A; holding the request past the fill's end
  returns to the same part. So a fill is also the door between two variations,
  and can start at any beat. (practice)
- **Parameter locks**: a lock is one setting's value for one step only; the
  sound returns to the kit's own value on the next step unless that step has a
  lock too. A lock can also slide toward the next. Kits hold sounds, patterns
  hold steps and locks.
- **The 1980s sampled sound**: short, low-resolution samples of real drums, a
  little grainy, made huge by a reverb cut off suddenly on the snare.
  (practice)
- **Chance in a pattern**, as IDM uses it: a line of sixteenths in which most
  steps sound one time in ten and the last two of each bar always, with
  loudness drawn at random over a wide range; a held note fed to a random
  arpeggiator two octaves wide; soft ghost kicks before or after the kick on
  each bar's first beat; clap, snare and a third sound stacked on the
  off-beats, each with its own varying loudness; a short clap fill closing a
  four-bar loop; a fast tempo, near 160. (practice)

## Electronic Percussion by Style

- **Dance music (house, techno, trance)**: a kick on every beat; a clap or
  snare on two and four; an open hat on every off-beat; a closed hat or shaker
  in sixteenths, accented for bounce; a ride added at the high point. Parts
  enter and leave every eight or sixteen bars. A build-up is a snare roll that
  doubles its speed, from quarters to eighths to sixteenths to thirty-seconds,
  with a rising sound and the kick taken out, then a beat of silence, then
  everything at once. House is straight or lightly swung; garage-like styles
  swing the hats hard. Every layer should have a job; a layer nobody would
  miss goes. (practice)
- **Electro**: no kick on every beat. A syncopated kick, snare or clap on two
  and four, cowbell and tom figures, stiff and exact.
- **Breakbeat styles (drum and bass, big beat)**: a drummer's recorded bar or
  two cut into slices and rearranged; the snare on two and four survives the
  cutting, and the kick moves. Ghost notes come with the break for free.
- **IDM and glitch**: the grid is the material. Steps repeat very fast
  (ratchets, rolls that speed up or slow down), single hits are cut, reversed,
  pitched and stuttered, patterns of different lengths run against each other
  and drift, steps play by chance or only on some passes, and a bar may change
  length. A steady element, often the tune or a pad, holds the listener while
  the drums misbehave. Push and pull against the grid and odd groupings
  (threes and fives across four) replace the backbeat. (practice)
- **1980s pop and rock with a machine**: the pattern is a drummer's,
  simplified and exact: a strong backbeat, even hats, a fill at each eight
  bars, claps doubling the snare in choruses. The exactness is the sound;
  humanizing it loses the period. (judgment)

## What This Suggests for llamatron

- A drummer in the wizard could be a **machine** as well as a person: exact
  timing, few loudnesses, accent and swing in place of ghost notes and push or
  drag, A and B variations with a fill for each. (judgment)
- Swing wants the feel values between straight and triplet, named in percent
  as the machines name them. (judgment)
- Rolls that change speed, steps by chance, steps on some passes only, and
  patterns of unequal length are the IDM toolkit, and llamatron's generators
  already hold several. (judgment)
- A kit is a choice apart from a pattern: the same pattern on another
  machine's sounds is another genre. (judgment)
