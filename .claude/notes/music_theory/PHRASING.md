# Phrasing: Lines, Sections and Their Contrast

How a melody divides into lines a listener hears as lines, how each kind of
section lays its lines out, and checks a program can run on a written melody.
It builds on MELODY.md ("Phrasing A Line", "Breath"), HOOKS.md and
SONG_STRUCTURE.md and does not repeat them. Certainty: **measured**,
**practice**, **judgment**, as the README says.

## What a Line Is

- A line is one breath and one phrase: in 4/4 pop typically 2 bars, 4 at slow
  tempos, so an 8-bar section is four lines (practice). Folk-song phrases
  average about nine and a half notes, from 2 to 21, so a usual line is 6 to
  13 notes and a short one 2 to 5 (measured).
- A line is counted two ways: its onsets, ties not counted, and its stresses,
  the notes on strong beats or an eighth before one. Lines of matching stress
  counts feel stable, mismatched ones push forward; once a third line repeats
  the first's pattern, the fourth is expected to repeat the second's; an odd
  count of lines feels unstable (practice).
- A boundary is heard where the gap after a note is clearly larger than the
  gaps around it, or where register, loudness, articulation or note length
  changes (practice, from the grouping theory of tonal music). Evenly spaced
  notes with nothing changing make a stream with no line in it: the commonest
  machine fault (judgment).
- The rule a program keeps: from a line's last onset to the next line's first,
  at least twice the line's median gap between onsets, of which at least half
  a beat is silence, or a last note at least twice the median length
  (judgment).

## Line-Length Patterns

| Pattern | Shape | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Line and tag | a long line (6–9 onsets) answered by a short one (3–5), twice or four times | punchy verses: theme songs, marches, novelty, call and response (judgment) |
| Matched pairs | two lines of one length, the second ending otherwise | a plain verse, the period's way (practice) |
| AABA lines | four lines of one length, the third the only change | verse or chorus (practice) |
| Short, short, long | two short units, then one about twice as long | the sentence in a line; strong for a chorus or a verse's end (practice) |
| Parallel climb | two lines of one rhythm, the second higher | a pre-chorus (practice) |
| Hook and run | a short hook (1–3 onsets) and its answer (1–3), then a long running line (7–10), twice, then a tag | a chorus with a shouted hook and a running body (judgment) |
| Cells | one cell struck three or four times, then a held note | a bridge's stutter, a rebuild (practice) |

- The sentence (practice): an idea of two bars and its repeat over the tonic,
  then a continuation that fragments into one-bar units, quickens the surface
  rhythm and the chords, and ends in a cadence, in proportion 1, 1, 2. In pop
  the same shape runs statement, restatement, departure, conclusion, the last
  two longer than either first, often 4 + 4 + 8 bars; the departure grew into
  the pre-chorus and the conclusion into the chorus, chords moving every two
  bars, then every bar, then every half bar. A program's rule: the first two
  units share a rhythm, the third halves the line length and doubles the chord
  rate, the fourth lands.
- The period (practice): a four-bar line ending weakly, usually on V, then one
  that repeats its opening and ends strongly. A program's rule: two lines with
  one first half, the first ending on degree 2, 5 or 7 over V, the second on 1
  or 3 over I.

## Where Lines Start and End

- Starts (practice): a pickup of one to three unstressed eighths, which belongs
  to the line after it, the breath coming before it; on the beat; or delayed to
  the and of one or to beat two. A verse often starts delayed or with a pickup
  and its chorus on the downbeat or just before it: a change of start is a mark
  of a new section. A line whose first stress falls on a weak beat sounds less
  settled, good in a verse, wrong for a hook.
- Ends (practice): a strong close, degree 1 on a strong beat, the line's
  longest note, over I; a weak close, degree 2, 5 or 7, or 3 over another
  chord, or a short note and a rest. Cadences prefer strong beats and long notes
  begin on them; players lengthen a line's end and its peak (measured).
- The last note of a long line is its longest, a beat or more, usually two; a
  tag's last note may be short and detached, which makes it punchy (judgment).
  An ending on the weak beat after a strong one is soft and suits a verse; one on
  the strong beat closes, for hooks and the ends of sections (practice).

## How a Tag Works (judgment, on call-and-response practice)

- Two to five onsets in a bar or less, one to four beats long, filling the hold
  or rest after a long line, starting one to two beats after its last onset,
  never across it unless another voice sings it.
- Punchier than the line it answers: more notes on or just before beats,
  detached, often ending short and accented before a rest.
- One rhythm every time in a section, its pitch changing at most on the third;
  a narrow range of one to three pitches near the line's last note.
- It comments on or confirms the line; it may carry the cadence, so the line
  ends weakly and the tag closes. It is the verse's own small hook, and differs
  in rhythm from the chorus's.

## Density and Contrast Between Sections

- Measures a program reads from any section: onsets a bar, onsets a line and
  the pattern of line lengths, the share of rest, the median gap and longest
  note, the share of notes two beats or longer, the share syncopated, the pitch
  centre and range, where lines start, and the share of lines whose rhythm
  matches another's.

| Section | Lines | Onsets a line | Onsets a bar | Rest | Long notes | Centre above the verse |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Verse | line and tag, or matched pairs | line 6–9, tag 3–5 | 3–6 | 20–40% | few, one held a long line | 0 |
| Pre-chorus | two parallel lines, maybe a gap | 5–7 each, one rhythm | 3–5, rising in the last bar | 15–30%, a beat's gap before the chorus | ends held | 2–4 semitones, rising |
| Chorus | hook, answer, running lines, tag | hook 1–3, answer 1–3, run 7–10, tag 3–5 | about the verse's average, but mixed | 15–30% | many on the hook and the lines' ends | 3–7 semitones |
| Bridge | cells, calls and answers, a question, a lead-in | cells 1–4, calls 2–5 | unlike the verse's | high | the held note after the stutter | away from the chorus |

All these numbers are judgment, to be tuned by ear.

- Shorter phrases raise intensity and long ones relax: a verse of four-bar
  lines and a chorus of two-bar ones is common, and the reverse works too; the
  principle is contrast, not one direction (practice). A busy verse can be
  followed by a spacious chorus and the reverse (practice).
- A chorus's notes are not all long: the hook is held and the body runs, so its
  note lengths gather at both ends, few very long and many short, a mix the
  verse lacks (judgment; choruses are often no slower than their verses,
  practice).
- Register alone never makes a chorus: one study of recordings found pitch
  height tells a chorus weakly next to its sound (measured); it goes with the
  line pattern, the hook and the arrangement (judgment).
- A chorus lands (practice): its hook on the first or last line, best both; one
  or two onsets a note on strong beats and the longest notes; running lines
  around it carrying the many notes; a gap or drop before it; its first note on
  the downbeat or an eighth before. With no words, the hook's held note is a
  downbeat of a bar or half bar, a short pickup before it (judgment).

## Hooks, Answers and Builds

- Call and response (practice): a phrase answered by another, in voices,
  instruments or both. Forms a program makes (judgment): a hook and its answer
  in one bar, the hook ending long or accented in the first half, the answer
  lower or level and quieter in the second; a line and its tag; a lead answered
  by a group; a lead answered by an instrument in its holds, never across its
  notes.
- An echo repeats the call's last one to three notes, an octave or a step lower
  or level, softer, one or two beats after; an interjection is one or two short
  hits in a line's rest, on a beat or just before (judgment).
- A stutter strikes one cell three or four times on consecutive eighths or
  quarters, often on one pitch, then holds a higher or steadier note two beats
  or more; four hits build, three state, never more than four before a change
  (judgment). Repetition builds tension and the change that breaks it releases
  (practice).
- The rule of three: state, restate, change; or three times and a change on the
  fourth (practice).
- A pre-chorus builds (practice): two lines of one rhythm, the second a step to
  a third higher; units shrinking from two bars to one or one to half; the
  chord rate doubling, the tonic avoided, an ending on V or IV; each line's
  start and peak higher than the last's; a held last note or a silence before
  the chorus. Pre-choruses grew from about a third of hit verse-chorus songs in
  the late sixties to about three fifths by the late eighties (measured).
- A bridge contrasts (practice): fragments in place of full lines; calls traded
  with answers; a last line asking a question, ending on 2, 5 or 7 over V,
  rising, then a rest or a held V; a lead-in of one to four notes rising into
  the chorus's downbeat. Of rebuilding bridges counted in recent charts, most
  run sixteen bars in stages of four, the thinnest point about two thirds into
  the song and the arrival about four fifths, most reusing earlier material,
  the chorus most of all, a third ending on the tonic (measured).
- An ending delays its last hook by an extra line: the last running line again,
  a new line, a deceptive move to vi and the line again, or a bar added; then
  the hook lands on 1 as the song's longest note; a tag ending repeats the last
  line two or three times (practice).

## Stress Without Words

- Stressed syllables are higher, louder and longer, and composers set them on
  strong beats, long notes off weak ones (measured, practice).
- In rock melodies (measured): stresses fall most on beat 1 (about a fifth),
  then 3, then 2 and 4; about a quarter of stresses are syncopated at the
  eighth, a tenth at the sixteenth; nearly all are anticipations, an eighth
  early, most into the and of 4 and the and of 2; sixteenth syncopation nearly
  vanishes above about 120 BPM; notes come no closer than about 100 ms;
  nineteenth-century parlour songs had almost none.
- A program's rules (judgment): mark a line's stresses, about every other
  onset, before its pitches; put them on 1 and 3 or an eighth early; make the
  last stress, the goal, the longest note; unstressed onsets short on weak
  eighths. Syncopation by style: a march or hymn none, a ballad 5 to 15%, pop
  and rock 15 to 30%, funk and soul more; sixteenth syncopation only below
  about 120 BPM. A hook may anticipate its long note by an eighth; a march's
  never does. Speech's strongest stress comes near a phrase's end (practice).
- Grouping and meter (practice): no tiny groups; boundaries at larger gaps and
  at changes; stronger cues make larger boundaries; two equal halves preferred;
  parallel passages grouped alike and given parallel meter; cadences on stable
  beats. A tag differs from its line in articulation or note length, and has a
  gap before it, so it is heard as its own group (judgment).
- Hypermeter (practice): four-bar units heard as hypermeasures, bar 1 the
  strongest, bar 3 next; irregular lengths come from an overlap, one unit's last
  bar the next's first, or an added bar. A section starts on a hyperdownbeat; a
  held note, a chorus's first note or a hook's long note on hyperbeat 1 or 3; an
  added bar only where meant (judgment).
- Tension and release (practice, judgment): rising pitch, more onsets, shorter
  units, faster chords, repeated cells and a held V build; a long chord tone on a
  strong beat, degree 1 and falling density release. Every section ends either
  releasing or handing on, never neither (judgment).

## Line Kinds for a Program (judgment)

| Kind | Beats | Onsets | Start | End | Rest after | Register |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Long line | 6–8 | 6–10 | a pickup or delayed | held a beat or more | half a beat or more, or a tag fills it | the section's base |
| Tag | 2–4 | 2–5 | 1–2 beats after the line's last onset | short and accented, or held a beat | half a beat or more | near the line's last note |
| Hook | 2–4 | 1–3 | the downbeat or an eighth before | the section's longest note, 2 beats or more, a chord tone | half a beat or more | the section's top |
| Answer | 2–4 | 1–3 | after the hook's long note | short, lower or level | half a beat or more | under the hook |
| Parallel | 4–8 | 4–7 | as its pair | its pair's rhythm, 2–4 semitones higher | half a beat to a beat | rising |
| Cell | 2–4 | 3–4 alike | on the beat | then a held note of 2 beats or more | none | one pitch, then up |
| Call, response | 2–4 each | 2–5 | the response in the call's rest | | small | the response another voice |
| Question | 4–8 | 4–8 | any | 2, 5 or 7 over V, rising | a beat or more, or a held V | |
| Lead-in | 1–2 | 1–4 | ends on the next section's downbeat | | none | rising to it |
| Extra | 6–8 | as a long line | | weak, delaying the hook | half a beat or more | at or under the hook |

- Section shapes (judgment): a verse of long line and tag four times, or matched
  pairs in plain styles; a pre-chorus of two parallel lines, the second higher,
  ending held on V, a gap beat before the chorus; a chorus of hook and answer, a
  running line, hook and answer, a running line, a tag ending strong; a bridge
  of four cells into a held note, calls and responses, a question and a lead-in;
  an ending of the chorus with an extra line before the last hook, held a bar or
  more on 1.
- By style (judgment, the march's form practice): a march with no syncopation,
  starts on the beat or one pickup, dotted figures, four-bar lines in periods,
  cadences every eight bars, tags as three- or four-note fanfares, contrast by
  strain not by line; a pop chorus with the hook-and-run shape, a pickup an
  eighth early allowed, 15 to 30% syncopation, starting on or before the
  downbeat where the verse started late; a rap verse on a sixteenth grid at two
  to four onsets a beat, one-bar lines with the goal on beat 4, a breath every
  two to four bars, little pitch, its sung hook contrasting in everything, and
  the stream check relaxed for it alone; a ballad of two-bar lines that feel
  like four, long pickups, rests of a beat or more, many long notes, the chorus
  higher by a fifth to an octave at its peak with fewer onsets a line, tags as
  instrumental answers.

## Checks a Program Runs on a Written Melody (judgment, thresholds by ear)

1. Stream: divide each section into lines by the gap rule; fail where a run with
   no boundary passes two bars in a verse or pre-chorus, four elsewhere, or the
   rest share is under a tenth, rap aside.
2. Line lengths: where a section calls for line and tag, some line is 60% or
   less of the longest's onsets; where it calls for matched pairs, paired lines
   differ by two onsets at most.
3. A chorus has a short hook: within its first two bars, a unit of three onsets
   or fewer in a bar or less, ending on a note of two beats or more or an
   accented note before half a beat's rest, its rhythm returning twice or more,
   on the first or last line.
4. A chorus is not all held: outside an anthem or ballad, a line has six or more
   onsets with a median gap of half a beat or less.
5. Sections differ: each against the verse on onsets a bar (15%), the long-note
   share (0.15), the median pitch (3 semitones), the line pattern and the start;
   a chorus on three or more, a pre-chorus or bridge on two or more.
6. A pre-chorus rises: each line's start and peak a step above the last's, its
   last peak at or above the verse's and at or below the chorus's, its lines
   sharing four fifths of their onset places, their lengths not growing, ending
   on V or IV or off the tonic, held or before a gap.
7. A bridge contrasts: its rhythm sharing under half the hook's, its start
   unlike the chorus's, with repeated cells or call and response, ending weakly
   before a lead-in or a gap.
8. Stress: seven tenths of each line's stresses (its long notes and first notes
   after rests) on 1 and 3 or an eighth before; syncopation within the style's
   band.
9. Tags in a section share one rhythm, at most the third or last differing.
10. Peaks: the verse's below the chorus's, the pre-chorus's at most the chorus's,
    the song's highest note in a hook, or once in the bridge or the last chorus.
11. The ending: an extra line or bar before the last hook; the last note degree
    1, on a strong beat, the song's longest.
12. Each section releases or hands on: a strong close, a held V or IV, or a gap
    or lead-in after it.

- Melody corpora with section labels under open licenses could measure real
  verse-against-chorus values for checks 1 to 5, counting only and naming no
  song (judgment).
