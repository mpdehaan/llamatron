# Blues

The twelve-bar chorus throughout, three dominant-seventh chords, the tune's lowered third and seventh against them. The singer's
line takes about two bars and an instrument answers in the next two: call and answer is the form itself. Starts on the turnaround,
the last four bars of the twelve, and ends on a turnaround walked down to a held chord. (practice)

- **Shuffle** and **driving shuffle**: the long-short pair on every beat, the bass walking; the second harder on the backbeat.
- **Texas shuffle**: a looser, swinging shuffle with the guitar forward.
- **Slow blues**: twelve-eight time, room for long answers, the drums on the ride with soft ghost notes.
- **Jump blues**: fast, swung, horns riffing behind the singer.
- **Minor blues**: the same twelve bars on minor chords, darker and slower.

## Blues Rock

- A **power trio**, guitar, bass and drums, each free: the bass plays lines, not roots, and the drums answer the guitar. Louder
  and longer than the blues, the twelve bars kept. (practice)
- The lead's phrasing is the style: short clipped phrases with space after each, bends that rise to the note and stop short,
  a phrase of three notes repeated and turned, major and minor pentatonic mixed in one line, rhythm invented afresh each
  chorus. A solo builds over several choruses from low and sparse to high and dense. (practice)
- Heavier descendants add a riff under the verse in place of the shuffle, slower tempos and more weight, and open the middle
  for improvising. (practice)
- In the classic trio the bass is a second lead: it plays tunes against the guitar's, moves through its whole range, and under a
  solo its line is nearly a song of its own, while staying tied to the drums. The drummer comes from jazz: toms and cymbals as
  melody, patterns that answer the soloist, power throughout. Live, one song opens into fifteen minutes of three-way
  improvising. With no chord instrument, the bass's fullness is what keeps a guitar solo from sounding empty. (practice)
- **The Texas shuffle**: bass walking in quarter notes, the guitar hitting chords on the upbeats with a heavy down-up stroke
  while muting, so one guitar is bass, chords and percussion; swung hard; in a guitar-friendly key with open strings. A small
  stock of licks played with great force is enough: feeling over vocabulary. (practice)
- **Sacred steel**: a pedal steel guitar as the lead voice, from gospel, sliding and crying like a singer, over seventies
  funk grooves; long dance jams that climb and climb; family-band call and response. (practice)

## Kinds of Blues, and Three Lead Styles

- **Delta**: one player, acoustic, slide or fingers, the guitar answering the voice; bars added or dropped at will.
- **Chicago**: the Delta song amplified with a band: drums, bass, piano, a harmonica through an amplifier playing like a horn,
  slide guitar; riffs under the verse, stop-time verses (see `SONG_STRUCTURE.md`); loud and rough.
- **Texas**: the guitar is the star, single-string leads with jazz and swing in them, horns behind, a swinging shuffle.
- **The one-chord boogie**: no changes at all; a foot-stomped pulse and a low riff under half-spoken lines; the guitar rarely
  plays while the voice sounds; lines as long as the words need.
- **Three lead styles**: the melodic one (few notes, a singing vibrato from the wrist, each phrase an answer to the voice, from
  gospel, never playing while singing); the bending one (a guitar tuned low and strung backwards, huge slow bends of a tone and
  more, few notes, great weight, horns behind); the driving one (fast runs, hard attack, instrumentals with hooks, nearest rock).
