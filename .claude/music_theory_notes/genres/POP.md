# Pop

What chart pop does apart from rock, by era. Principles only; measures are from the chord dataset described in `HARMONY.md`.

## By Era

- **Early sixties writers' pop** (the song factories, girl groups, bubblegum, sunshine pop): major keys (minor in one song in
  seven), I IV and I vi IV V, ii in half of songs, the minor iv in a fifth, the major II before V in four in ten, bVI in a quarter
  to a third; sevenths on a tenth of chords. Intro in eight songs in ten, a chorus in seven in ten, a bridge in a third, almost no
  solos; an instrumental interlude instead. Written at the piano: a key change up a semitone for the last chorus is a trademark.
- **Seventies soft pop and singer-songwriters**: piano or acoustic guitar leads; ii and vi in half the songs, major sevenths,
  slash chords with a falling bass; 12- and 16-bar verses; fade endings.
- **Eighties pop and dance pop**: drum machines and synth bass; minor in a third to a half; bVII in four in ten; pre-choruses
  arrive; intros of 8 and 16 bars; the fade at its height.
- **Nineties and later pop**: minor in about half of songs. vi in three songs in four; bVII in one in eight; almost no sevenths,
  no solos (one song in a hundred), a bridge in six in ten. One four-chord loop for the whole song (I V vi IV, vi IV I V,
  i bVI bIII bVII, I vi IV V): verse, pre-chorus and chorus differ by tune, rhythm and texture, not by chords. The commonest
  whole shape is intro, verse, chorus, verse, chorus, bridge, chorus, in a tenth of all songs, more uniform than any other style.
  Intros shrink to two or four bars or none (an intro is labeled in six songs in ten).
- **Guitar pop of the nineties** (the British kind): I IV, I V, bVII in three in ten, a solo in one in eight, outro in half.
- **Chamber, baroque and dream pop**: I IV and I vi; fewer choruses (three in four songs), few bridges, long outros; strings,
  keyboards and reverb carry the interest.

## What Makes Pop Pop

- **The loop is fixed; the top line does the work.** With four chords throughout, contrast comes from the tune's rhythm and
  range, the density of the words, and instruments entering and leaving. (practice)
- **Melody by repetition**: a hook is a short cell, often three notes or one rhythm, stated, repeated, varied and resolved; many
  hooks are rhythm on one or two pitches. Syncopation is placed on the title. Phrases are four bars; verse phrases start after
  the beat and chorus phrases on it (or the reverse), so the two are told apart at once. (practice)
- **A new thing every few bars**: a part added, removed or changed every four or eight bars; the second verse never the same
  as the first; a drop to almost nothing before the last chorus.
- **The lift**: a pre-chorus that withholds the home chord, a bar of silence or a fill, then the chorus with everything in; a
  last chorus a semitone or tone higher in older pop, or with an added counter-melody in later pop.
- **Bass and drums**: four on the floor or a plain backbeat; the bass on roots in eighths or a syncopated synth figure; handclaps
  or a snare layered on 2 and 4; a tambourine or shaker in choruses.
