# Pop

What chart pop does apart from rock, by era. Principles only; measures are from the chord dataset described in `HARMONY.md`.

## By Era

- **Early sixties writers' pop** (the song factories, girl groups, bubblegum, sunshine pop): major keys (minor in one song in
  seven), I IV and I vi IV V, ii in half of songs, the minor iv in a fifth, the major II before V in four in ten, bVI in a quarter
  to a third; sevenths on a tenth of chords. Intro in eight songs in ten, a chorus in seven in ten, a bridge in a third, almost no
  solos; an instrumental interlude instead. Written at the piano: a key change up a semitone for the last chorus is a trademark.
- **Seventies soft pop and singer-songwriters**: piano or acoustic guitar leads; ii and vi in half the songs, major sevenths,
  slash chords with a falling bass; 12- and 16-bar verses; fade endings.
- **Eighties pop and dance pop**: drum machines and synth bass; minor in a third to a half; bVII in four in ten; pre-choruses
  arrive; intros of 8 and 16 bars; the fade at its height.
- **Nineties and later pop**: minor in about half of songs. vi in three songs in four; bVII in one in eight; almost no sevenths,
  no solos (one song in a hundred), a bridge in six in ten. One four-chord loop for the whole song (I V vi IV, vi IV I V,
  i bVI bIII bVII, I vi IV V): verse, pre-chorus and chorus differ by tune, rhythm and texture, not by chords. The commonest
  whole shape is intro, verse, chorus, verse, chorus, bridge, chorus, in a tenth of all songs, more uniform than any other style.
  Intros shrink to two or four bars or none (an intro is labeled in six songs in ten).
- **Guitar pop of the nineties** (the British kind): I IV, I V, bVII in three in ten, a solo in one in eight, outro in half.
- **Chamber, baroque and dream pop**: I IV and I vi; fewer choruses (three in four songs), few bridges, long outros; strings,
  keyboards and reverb carry the interest.

## What Makes Pop Pop

- **The loop is fixed; the top line does the work.** With four chords throughout, contrast comes from the tune's rhythm and
  range, the density of the words, and instruments entering and leaving. (practice)
- **Melody by repetition**: a hook is a short cell, often three notes or one rhythm, stated, repeated, varied and resolved; many
  hooks are rhythm on one or two pitches. Syncopation is placed on the title. Phrases are four bars; verse phrases start after
  the beat and chorus phrases on it (or the reverse), so the two are told apart at once. (practice)
- **A new thing every few bars**: a part added, removed or changed every four or eight bars; the second verse never the same
  as the first; a drop to almost nothing before the last chorus.
- **The lift**: a pre-chorus that withholds the home chord, a bar of silence or a fill, then the chorus with everything in; a
  last chorus a semitone or tone higher in older pop, or with an added counter-melody in later pop.
- **Bass and drums**: four on the floor or a plain backbeat; the bass on roots in eighths or a syncopated synth figure; handclaps
  or a snare layered on 2 and 4; a tambourine or shaker in choruses.

## Writers' Pop of the Early Sixties

Habits of the professional songwriters, girl groups and vocal duos before the beat groups. (practice)

- **The sophisticated writer**: the tune leads and the bar lines follow: a bar of five, seven or two beats dropped into a song
  in four wherever the words need it, so a phrase never waits. Melody notes sit on the chord's seventh, ninth or eleventh; major
  sevenths and suspended chords; wide leaps, then steps back. Soft brass (a mellow horn, muted trombone), strings and a light
  Latin beat under a soul voice. A song can change key for each section.
- **The wall of sound**: no instrument is heard alone. Three to five players double every part (several guitars strumming the
  same chords, two or three pianos, two basses), percussion in layers (castanets, sleigh bells, tambourine, shakers), strings on
  top, all in one echoing room. The drum figure is a kick on one, the and of two and three, with the snare on four only. The
  texture is one mass, so the arrangement changes by adding the mass, not by solos.
- **The operatic ballad**: a song that never returns to a section: each part new, each higher and louder, ending on the top note;
  or four verses in a row over a bolero rhythm, each fuller, then one new climactic part. No chorus. The voice starts low and
  quiet and ends in falsetto or a held high note; the band grows from a single guitar to strings, voices and full drums.
- **The close-harmony duo**: two voices a third apart singing the same words in the same rhythm, each line a tune by itself;
  acoustic guitars loud and percussive, strummed with a ticking muted rhythm, an open tuning with a capo; country songs with a
  rock and roll beat.
- **The falsetto group**: a falsetto lead over three voices on vowels, the top and bottom harmony notes an octave apart; a
  stomping beat with handclaps; songs as small stories; doo-wop's I vi IV V made loud.
- **The piano writer**: gospel and soul under pop: IV over V in the bass (a suspended dominant) in place of a plain V, the
  left hand walking, the right hand hammering chords in rhythm.

## Mid-Sixties to Seventies Pop, More

- **The jazz-minor beat group**: minor keys, an electric piano or organ soloing in a jazz manner, breathy quiet singing, close
  three-part harmony; later a tape keyboard for strings and flutes, and baroque touches.
- **The piano man, gospel kind**: the left hand in octaves, or root, fifth, root, acting as the kick drum; the right as the snare,
  chords pushed on the off-beats; slash chords everywhere (a chord over another bass note, the bass walking down under a held
  chord); suspended dominants and secondary dominants from gospel; broken-chord intros that are hooks.
- **The piano man, classical kind**: tunes and inner voices shaped like nineteenth-century piano music; ii V I and sevenths from
  jazz; each song a different borrowed style; a piano intro that is the hook; craft over groove.
- **The soft-pop arranger**: one voice low and close; harmony stacks of four parts each sung three times; an electric piano
  doubling the acoustic piano; woodwinds, harp and strings taking turns; drums plain and behind. Every chorus adds a layer.
- **The steady pop-rock band**: bass and kick as one, never busy, the bass singing a short tune between chords; a fingerpicked
  guitar pattern in place of strumming; three sections built from the same chords by changing only who plays; three singers'
  harmony.
- **The orchestral pop band**: strings as riffs, not pads; a falling bass or chromatic inner line under the chords; a dry,
  close, heavy drum sound; stacked voices and a voice through a vocoder; borrowed minor iv and flat six.
- **The one-man soul-pop band**: the drums recorded first by the writer, loose and pushing; a synth bass; a funk keyboard played
  like percussion in several interlocking tracks; chords that start away from home and move by chromatic step; ninths,
  elevenths, suspended sevenths; a top note held while the chords change beneath; keys awkward for guitars; a short bridge that
  breaks the riff.
- **Rock and soul duo**: soul singing over a sparse eighties rhythm track, the piano playing parts and not chords, room left
  round the snare.
