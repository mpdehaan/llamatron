# Soul and Funk

The groove is the song: drums and bass locked, every other part a small rhythm in the gaps. Horns answer the singer and punch
accents. Few chords, sometimes one. Ghost notes everywhere. (practice)

- **Sixties soul**: a drum fill in, a steady backbeat, horns answering, a tag ending.
- **Southern soul**: slower and heavier, the backbeat laid back, horns in long notes and stabs.
- **Soul ballad**: keys first, twelve-eight or slow four, the band swelling at the chorus.
- **Funk**: drums first, kicks off the beat, sixteenth-note guitar scratches, the first beat of the bar hit hard by all; a hard stop.
- **New orleans**: a rolling, marching snare feel between straight and swung, piano forward.
- **Disco**: kick on every beat, open hi-hat on the off-beats, octave bass, strings; layers in and layers out.

## More Soul and Funk

- **Memphis soul**: two singers in call and response from church; horns that play percussion, short stabs and lines, not
  tunes; drums a metronome for the stabs; a dead, woody bass out of the kick's way; sparse, made on the spot. (practice)
- **Organ-led beat soul**: build the intro one part at a time, bass riff, then drums, guitar stabs, then a distorted organ
  riff; the bass line repeats under everything. (practice)
- **Funk rock**: a screaming, sustained lead guitar over a steady, unhurried pocket of bass and keys; soft-rock songs remade as
  funk. (practice)
- **The big funk collective**: everything starts from the first beat of the bar, hit together, then the in-between notes are
  layered; a groove more than a song, many small parts interlocking; a synthesizer bass fatter than a bass guitar; jazz horn
  lines written tightly; chants. (practice)
- **Horn bar band**: fifties and sixties rhythm and blues with eighties polish, horn section, harmonica, group harmonies.
- **Eighties synth funk**: drum machine, a dry close synth bass line, minor key, a sung hook by a second voice. (thin)

## Sixties Soul, More

- **Three houses.** The Detroit kind: written at the piano, chords rich (ii, iii, the minor iv, sevenths on a fifth of chords),
  a busy melodic bass with chromatic passing notes that never repeats a bar exactly, a snare or tambourine on every beat or
  a heavy backbeat doubled by handclaps, vibraphone and strings, baritone saxophone under the horns, a semitone key change for the
  last chorus. The Memphis kind: plain triads, the band playing less, horns in unison lines and held pads, a delayed backbeat, organ
  and guitar answering the singer. The Alabama studio kind: the singer's own gospel piano sets the feel and the band follows it;
  an electric piano riff beside it; rhythm section and voice cut together, horns and backing voices added after.
- **Gospel in the piano**: walk-ups harmonized in thirds or sixths, IV with the home note kept in the bass, passing diminished
  chords between I and ii or IV and V, secondary dominants, the IV to I "amen" close, sevenths and suspensions as color, fills in
  the singer's rests. Slow songs in 6/8 or 12/8 with triplet piano chords.
- **Stop time**: the band stops on beat one and the singer or the backing voices fill the bar alone, once in a song, late.
- **Call and response** between the lead and three backing voices, the answer a fixed short phrase; a vamp at the end where the
  lead improvises over the voices' repeated line as the track fades.
- **The family gospel group gone soul**: one electric guitar with heavy tremolo, fingerpicked, country-blues phrasing, setting the
  groove and the key by itself; voices in close family harmony above, a low lead voice; later a Memphis rhythm section under it, the
  bass playing a two-bar figure that is the hook, a blues progression, an organ from church.
- Measured: a chorus first in a sixth of songs; a bridge in four in ten; a solo in one song in fifteen; I IV the leading loop,
  then I ii, I vi IV V and I vi ii V.

## Disco, More

Measures are from the chord dataset. (measured, practice)

- **The beat**: kick on every beat at 110 to 130; snare or clap on 2 and 4; closed hi-hat in eighths or sixteenths with the open
  hi-hat on every off-beat, closing on the beat; extra kicks and ghost snares for lift; congas, tambourine and a shaker above.
  Toms or a rising snare roll into sections. The beat never stops, so the arrangement does the changing.
- **The bass**: octaves, low on the beat and high off it, or a syncopated two-bar figure that moves by step and chromatic approach
  into each chord; as much rhythm as tune. Synth bass in sixteenths in the European and later kinds.
- **The guitar**: a clean, thin sound playing small chords on the top three or four strings in sixteenths, most strokes muted
  scratches and a few let sound, never every sixteenth; a second guitar on single-note lines or high octaves. The parts lock into
  the hi-hat.
- **Keys, strings and horns**: electric piano or piano on rich chords; a funk keyboard doubling the guitar's rhythm; strings in
  long high lines, falling runs into a chorus and stabs answering the voice; horns punching with the kick. Sevenths and
  extensions on a fifth of chords; ii in six songs in ten, iii in half, the minor iv in a fifth, bVII in a third.
- **Keys and chords**: minor in a third of songs (nearly half in the later kind, six in ten in the European kind). Loops: I IV, i v,
  i IV, I vi ii V; the European kind i v, bVI bVII i, i bVI bVII with plain triads. Bridges start on bVI or IV, or drop IV to iv.
- **Shape**: intro in three songs in four, long enough to mix (8 or 16 bars of beat, parts entering one by one); verse, chorus,
  verse, chorus; a chorus first in a sixth of songs; a bridge in under four in ten; almost no solos (one song in sixteen): in a
  solo's place a **break**, everything out but drums and percussion, then bass, then the rest returning in layers. A key change up
  for the last choruses in the poppier kind. The extended version doubles the intro and the break.
- **The pop-songwriter kind** (a vocal group that came to disco from sixties pop): a falsetto lead with close three-part harmony
  stacked above it; verses low and rhythmic, almost spoken on few notes, choruses high and sustained; a pre-chorus that climbs;
  songs written like pop ballads (major sevenths, a falling bass, a minor iv) and then set to the beat; a rhythm track built on a
  bar or two of drums repeated exactly, so the feel is machine-steady; a funk keyboard or synth bass playing the hook figure;
  strings and horns answering each vocal line. Its ballads are slow soul with electric piano and the same harmony stack.
- **Nearby kinds**: the Philadelphia kind before it (strings, vibraphone, iii and ii chords, the hi-hat pattern's origin); the
  fast electronic kind after it (sequenced octave bass, minor keys, an instrumental section in a quarter to half of songs).

## Funk, More

- **The puzzle**: every player has a short, simple part that repeats exactly and leaves most of the bar empty; the parts fit
  into each other's holes, and the groove is their sum. Nobody plays the whole rhythm. All hit the first beat of the bar hard,
  then scatter. One chord for the whole song, with one move to a second chord for the bridge, entered on a shouted cue. The
  space left is where the singer goes. Horn lines can be borrowed from modal jazz: two notes, answered.
- **The New Orleans quartet**: the drummer plays parade rhythms on the kit, the accents falling on a clave-like pattern of
  three hits then two, snare and hi-hat syncopated against each other so the beat feels pulled apart and put back; guitar and
  organ play clipped riffs in unison or in answer; the bass doubles the riff; everything sparse, mid tempo, mostly instrumental.
  When one player steps forward, the drummer drops back to the plain groove.
- **Horn sections**: unison or octaves nine times in ten; chords saved for the hits; close voicings for stabs, open ones for
  swells and falls. The trumpet-led kind is bright and high; the kind with a baritone saxophone adds low spat notes in
  counterpoint under the stabs, and interlocking horn rhythms under a verse. Sixteenth-note bass lines played with the fingers,
  every note short, lock under them.
