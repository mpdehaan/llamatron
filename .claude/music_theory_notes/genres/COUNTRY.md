# Country and Bluegrass

Not a genre of the wizard yet. (practice, from lessons)

- **Boom-chick**: bass note on one and three alternating root and fifth, a strum of two strings on two and four.
- **Train beat**: snare brushed in steady eighths or sixteenths with accents on two and four, like a train; the outlaw kind
  leans on the downbeat and drives.
- **Bluegrass**: no drums; guitar and bass are the foundation, the mandolin chops the backbeat as the snare would, banjo rolls
  in steady notes, fiddle and resonator guitar weave fills and trade solos; high close harmony.

## Kinds of Country

(practice, from lessons and histories; the outlaw kind's details are from knowledge, the searches being thin)

- **The train trio**: the electric guitar plays single low notes, palm-muted, alternating root and fifth, with a muted strum
  between ("boom-chick-a"); the low strings damped so it never fights the bass; upright bass on the same notes; no drums at
  first, the rhythm made by the guitars alone, like a train. The tune's simplicity is the style: almost no fills.
- **Honky tonk and the shuffle**: a swung four with a walking bass, or a two-beat with the bass on one and three; brushed
  snare; fiddle and pedal steel trading fills between sung lines and answering the voice; the guitar forward with twangy leads.
  The intro is the back half of the chorus played by a lead instrument.
- **The smooth city kind**: fiddles and barroom piano removed; strings, a backing choir on vowels, soft horns; the steel
  swelling like a string section; a muted, picked bass doubling the upright an octave up for a click on each note; pop song forms.
- **The outlaw kind**: a small road band recorded plainly; a heavy two-beat that leans on the downbeat with the bass loud and
  simple; a guitar through a swirling effect playing low twangy leads; or a nylon-string guitar with jazz chords and a singer
  phrasing far behind the beat, catching up at line ends. Rock and soul rhythm sections under country songs. Verses in a row
  with a refrain; a bridge in one song in six. (measured)
- **Bluegrass roles**: bass on the beats and guitar's boom-chick are the floor; the mandolin's muted chop is the backbeat (the
  fiddle or banjo takes it when the mandolin leads); the banjo rolls quietly behind singing and loudly in a break; the fiddle
  plays long notes or shuffles behind the voice. Breaks pass around the band, each a verse long, the others dropping to backup;
  a kickoff and an ending are short breaks. An instrumental break in a third of songs. (measured)
- **Nineties arena country**: rock drums, heavy and with reverb on the chorus; a lean electric lead guitar; fiddle and steel
  kept for the fills; acoustic guitars strummed throughout; verse quiet, chorus lifted; a bridge in over half the songs. (measured)
