# Arpeggios and Sequenced Lines

- An arpeggiator takes held notes and plays them one at a time. Its settings are few and each is a musical decision: **rate**
  (sixteenths the default, eighths for weight, triplets for a roll; straight, dotted or triplet), **order** (up, down, up and
  down, as played, random), **range** (one octave circles in place, two climbs and falls, three or four sweeps), **gate** (short
  for a pluck, long for a wash; over the full step the notes overlap), and **swing**. (practice)
- An arpeggio is harmony turned into rhythm: one chord becomes a driving pulse without a drummer. A slow tempo with fast arpeggio
  notes gives calm and motion at once, which is the sound of the synthesizer score: a heartbeat pulse far below, sixteenths above.
  (practice)
- **The arpeggio as the theme**: one chord, often a seventh chord that is not the home chord, rising and falling in even notes,
  repeated without change for the whole piece. The line is tune and accompaniment together. Since the notes never change, all
  movement comes from the sound: a filter opening slowly, a second sound layered in, a bass note entering under it and changing
  what the same arpeggio means. (practice)
- **The ostinato score**: a short figure in a minor key, often in an odd meter such as five, repeated on a piano or a synthesizer
  over a held low note, with very few cues used sparingly. Closer to rock and pop in its plainness than to an orchestra; made
  fast and cheap, and frightening because it does not stop or develop. (practice)
- **The octave bass**: the chord's root jumping between two octaves on every eighth or sixteenth, the engine of italo, hi-nrg and
  synthwave; the same thing as an arpeggiator on one held note with a range of two octaves. (practice)
- **Against the bar**: an arpeggio of three or five notes in sixteenths drifts across a bar of four and returns, a cheap
  and very effective source of interest; holding four notes makes it square. (practice)
- **In a song**: arpeggios thin a texture where chords would thicken it, sit well under a voice at a small range, and lift a
  chorus when the range or the rate rises. Changing the held chord while the pattern keeps running is the usual way a
  progression is played. (practice)
- What changes over time is what keeps a repeated line alive: brightness, range, gate, which octave, and what enters under
  and over it. (judgment)
