# Bass Lines

The ways a bass part is written, by family, each named for what it sounds like, so that any style may borrow any of them. A way
is a rule for which note of the chord sounds where in the bar; a genre is a habit of choosing some ways over others.

## What Every Bass Line Decides

- **Which notes**: the root alone; root and fifth; root, fifth and octave; the chord's tones; the scale between them; notes
  outside the scale that lean into the next chord. More kinds of note is more melody and less weight. (practice)
- **Which beats**: the first beat of a chord nearly always takes the root. After that a line is on the beats (steady), between
  them (pushing or bouncing), or both. Beats one and three matter most, since chords change there. (practice)
- **How long**: held notes are weight and calm; short notes with gaps are funk and bounce; a ghost, a muted note with no clear
  pitch, keeps time between real notes. Where a note stops is as much the part as where it starts. (practice)
- **The join**: the last beat, or last half beat, before a chord changes is where a line leads in: a half step above or below the
  new root, a scale step, or the new chord's fifth. A line with no joins sounds programmed. (practice)
- **With the kick**: the bass sounds where the kick sounds and rests or moves where the kick does not; in dance music the two
  take turns instead, the bass between the kicks. (practice)
- **Register**: an electric bass is strongest from its lowest note up about an octave and a half; above that it sings and stops
  carrying. A line climbs for a chorus or a fill and comes home. (practice)
- **By section**: sparser and longer under a verse so the singer has room, driving eighths under a chorus, a climb or a stop into
  each new section, something new in the bridge. (practice)

## Root Ways (weight)

- **Held roots**: one note a chord, or one a bar. Ballads, slow rock, the first verse of anything.
- **Roots on the beats**: four to the bar, plain. A march; a verse that leaves room.
- **Pumping eighths**: the root in even eighths, all one loudness or the beats a little louder. The engine of rock, punk and new
  wave; played with a pick it is sharper. Its one freedom is the join: the last eighth or two before a change step into the
  next root. A chord's root landed an eighth early pushes the change without changing the rhythm.
- **Pushing roots**: roots that land on the and before the beat, ahead of the band or with the kick's pushes.
- **The gallop**: an eighth and two sixteenths on one note, over and over. Fast heavy styles.
- **The pedal**: one note held or repeated while the chords change above it, home or the fifth. Tension that the release, the
  bass finally moving, pays off. (See `TECHNIQUES.md`.)
- **Root then rest**: the root on one, silence after; the space is the hook. Early pop ballads, reggae's kin.

## Root and Fifth Ways (bounce)

- **Two-beat**: root on one, fifth on three (the fifth below where there is room). Country, polka, old-time pop, a ballad's
  walk. With an eighth-note pickup into each new chord it is the pop standard.
- **Root, fifth, octave**: the same reaching up to the octave, on the beats or as a bar-long figure. Bright and open.
- **Octaves**: the root jumping between two octaves, low on the beat and high off it, in eighths (disco, dance pop) or
  sixteenths (hi-nrg, italo). The high note is short. (See `ARPEGGIOS.md`.)
- **The tumbao kind**: the root avoided on one after the first bar; notes on the and of two and on four, tied over the bar line,
  root, fifth and octave. The line pulls the band forward. Latin styles and the funk that borrowed from them.

## Walking Ways (motion)

- **The walk**: one note a beat, even, never stopping. Beat one the root; beats two and three chord or scale tones; beat four a
  note that leans into the next root, from a half step above or below, a scale step, or the fifth of the chord coming. With two
  chords in a bar, each gets its root and one leading note. A line mostly moves by step and changes direction after a leap.
  Jazz, swing, jump blues, honky tonk.
- **The two-feel**: the walk at half speed, notes on one and three, a skip before some. A jazz tune's first chorus before the
  walk begins; switching from two to four is a lift the whole band feels.
- **The boogie**: a bar-long figure up the chord and back, root, third, fifth, sixth (and flat seventh), in eighths or swung
  eighths, the same shape moved to each chord. Boogie-woogie, rock and roll, early rhythm and blues.
- **Walking down** (or up): the scale stepped from one chord's root to the next over a bar or two, one note a beat or a bar.
  Under held chords it is a tune of its own: a ballad's falling bass.
- **Roots with a climb**: plain roots, and a run up the scale or chromatically in the last bar of a phrase into the next.

## Riff Ways (identity)

- **Doubling the riff**: the bass plays the guitar's or the keyboard's riff an octave below, note for note. Hard rock and metal
  verses; the two together are the riff.
- **Its own riff** (ostinato): a figure of one or two bars repeated through a section or a song while the chords above change or
  do not. The song is recognized by it. Funk, soul, groove jazz, dance music, some rock.
- **Riff and answer**: the figure in bar one, a variation or a rest in bar two; a four-bar riff is often one bar three times and
  a turnaround.
- **The gritty riff**: a short figure of few notes on a distorted or resonant synth bass, the timbre moving more than the notes.

## Melodic Ways (a second tune)

- **Melodic and busy**: a line that never repeats exactly, with passing notes, chromatic runs into chords, syncopation and ghost
  notes, under a simple song. The soul and pop of the 1960s. It still lands roots on one.
- **Counter-melody**: a tune in the bass's upper register that moves when the singer holds and holds when the singer moves.
  Later 1960s pop and its heirs.
- **Fills at the joins**: a plain line that breaks into a short run only in the last beat or two of a phrase.

## Syncopated Ways (funk)

- **On the one, then syncopated**: a heavy root on beat one of every bar, then short notes and ghosts on sixteenths around the
  beats, and silence; the bar's shape repeats. Funk.
- **Sparse and syncopated**: two or three notes a bar, placed off the beat, long gaps. The gaps are for the drums.
- **Slap and pop**: the low note struck with the thumb on the beat, a high note (often the octave) snapped between; percussive,
  two voices from one player. Funk, disco, later pop.
- **Two notes with gaps**: a pair of short notes and a rest, the rest longer than the notes. Electro, minimal dance music.

## Reggae and Dub Ways

- **Heavy and melodic with holes**: a tune-like figure of a bar or two in the low register, loud, with whole beats of silence,
  often resting on beat one while the drums drop there. The bass is the lead instrument; the guitar and keys keep time above.
  (What can be read disagrees on which beats are stressed; to check before building.)

## Dance Music Ways

- **Offbeat**: one short note between every pair of kicks. House, trance, hi-nrg. Bass and kick never sound together.
- **Rolling**: sixteenths between the kicks, three to a beat with the kick's place left empty, few pitches, loudness and length
  varying. Trance, tech house.
- **One note in sixteenths**: a machine pulse, the filter doing the moving.
- **The acid line**: see `genres/ELECTRONIC.md`, "The Acid Line in Detail": slides, accents and the filter are the part.
- **Long low notes**: a sub tone held a bar or more, moving in the gaps the drums leave. Dub techno, dubstep, drum and bass
  (the bass at half the drums' speed).
- **The wobble**: a held note whose filter opens and closes in a rhythm; the rhythm of the filter is the line.

## Keyboard and Organ Bass

- **Left hand bass**: with no bass player the keyboardist's left hand plays the line on a keyboard bass or the organ's lower
  keyboard, so the line stays within a hand's reach: roots, fifths, octaves and short walks, few leaps. (See `KEYBOARDS.md`.)
- **The organ walk**: a jazz organist walks the bass with the left hand, legato, with grace notes fingered into the line; the
  foot taps one pedal lightly on each beat only to give the notes a thump, as a drummer feathers the kick. A few walk the
  pedals themselves, smooth, heel and toe. (measured)
- **Root and fifth on the pedals**: the rock and pop organist of the 1960s plays root and fifth with the left foot, very little
  walking. A small pedalboard of one octave limits a line to that. (measured)
- **Gospel**: the pedals kick the root on one and three while the left hand walks above them; moving from the two-beat to the
  walk is the lift. (measured)
- **The pedal tone**: an organ's held low note under moving chords, for a hymn, an intro or a build.
- **The descending bass**: see `KEYBOARDS.md`.

## Choosing a Way

- A style has a home way and a few neighbors: rock, pumping eighths and doubling the riff; pop, two-beat with pickups and the
  melodic kinds; soul, melodic and busy; funk, on the one; country, two-beat; jazz, the two-feel and the walk; blues, the
  boogie and the walk; dance, offbeat, octaves and rolling.
- Borrowing a way from another style is a known move and changes a song's genre more than any other single part: a walk under
  rock chords is rockabilly, octaves under rock are disco rock, a pedal under pop is tension, a tumbao under soul is Latin soul.
- One song may use two or three ways by section: held roots in the verse, pumping eighths in the chorus, a walk in the bridge.
