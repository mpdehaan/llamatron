# Hooks

- A hook is whatever a listener carries away: usually a short phrase of melody with its words, but also a riff, a rhythm, a drum
  figure, a sound, or a stop. A song often has several: the chorus's sung hook, an instrumental hook heard in the intro and between
  sections, and a rhythmic hook in the groove. (practice)
- Tunes that stick tend to share three things: a quicker pace, an overall shape that is common and easy to follow (rising then
  falling, or the reverse), and inside that plain shape one or two unusual moves, a leap larger than expected or a repeated note
  where a step was expected. Ordinary enough to hold, odd enough to notice. (measured, as a tendency and not a recipe)
- Short is strong: a hook is commonly one or two bars, a handful of notes, with a rhythm that could be clapped and recognized. The
  rhythm identifies a hook at least as much as its pitches do. (practice)
- Repetition makes a hook, and exact repetition wears it out. The usual pattern is to state it, state it again, vary it (higher,
  or with a changed ending), and return: the shapes AABA and AAAB over four phrases. (practice)
- Placement: the sung hook at the start or the end of the chorus, on strong beats and long notes; the instrumental hook in the
  intro, after each chorus, and under the ending. Hearing the hook before the first verse makes the chorus feel familiar the first
  time it arrives. (practice)
- A hook needs room: the bars around it are plainer, other players thin out or double it, and the drums often mark its arrival.
  A second line fighting the hook weakens both. (practice)
- A hook should be singable: mostly steps and small leaps, within about an octave, sitting where an untrained voice is comfortable.
  (practice)

## Building a Sung Hook

- **A figure and its answer**: the hook proper is one bar or less, a few short notes that drive into one long note. The bar after
  answers it lower, with the same short notes in a looser order and a breath at the end. Figure and answer together make the
  line; the figure alone is what is remembered. (practice)
- **The long note is the point**: the figure's last note is its longest, at least half a bar, on a note of the chord, and it is
  the highest note of the song. A hook whose peak the verse has already used has nothing left to give. So the verse is written
  under a ceiling, and only the hook, a bridge or a pre-chorus's last climb may pass it. (practice)
- **Four ways into the peak**: one note repeated and then a leap up; a climb by step; the peak first, a fall away and a leap
  back; a turn about the two notes under it. The leap or the repeated note is the one odd move inside a plain shape. (practice)
- **Few pitches**: three or four in the figure. The verse may wander over more; the hook does not. (practice)
- **Across a chorus**: figure and answer, the same with the answer's last note risen to the peak or above, the first again, then
  a different closing line that comes down home. The rise in the second line is the lift; the plain third line is the return.
  Six bare repeats of a figure in eight bars wears it out; three, each answered, does not. (practice, and heard in trial tunes)
- **Length of notes is not the contrast**: measured on tunes made by rule, a hook's notes are not reliably longer on average than
  its verse's, since the short notes of the figure pull the average down. What tells verse from chorus is the height, the one
  long note, the fewer pitches and the exact return. (measured on generated tunes only)

## More Than One Hook

- A long or loosely played song wants several: the sung hook, an instrumental figure, and a returning theme that a jam leaves and
  finds again. Each must be short enough to be known after one hearing, and they should differ in rhythm before they differ in
  pitch. (practice)

## The Hook and Its Chorus

- The hook is a part of the chorus's melody, never the whole of it and never a thing beside it: the chorus is the frame built to
  show the hook. In a chorus of four lines the hook is the line that returns, said first, or last, or both, two to four times a
  chorus, each time the same but for a changed ending. (practice)
- The chorus's other lines serve the hook: plainer, lower, more by step, and never a second catchy figure. (practice)
- Across the song three things are essential: the sections before the chorus sit lower and narrower and move in shorter notes;
  nothing before the chorus reaches the hook's top note, though a bridge or a last chorus may pass it once; and the hook's
  rhythm is not used up elsewhere. Hearing the hook from an instrument before it is sung, and an instrument answering it in the
  chorus's gaps or the ending, are optional and good. (practice)
- A song has one main sung hook, which owns the title and the highest note. A second sung hook is a short tag or chant in a
  section after the chorus, at the end of the section before it, or in the ending, and differs from the main hook in shape: short
  and rhythmic against a long held note. Two sung hooks are the most a song carries. (practice)
- A riff is a hook an instrument plays, but is written and used differently: a figure looped under other things and belonging to
  a player, where a sung hook is a line heard a few times and belonging to a section. The two are kept apart on a page and
  treated as one family underneath: a riff differs from the main hook in rhythm, stays under its top, and thins out while the
  hook is sung. Two catchy figures never sound at once. (practice)
- What makes songs worse: a hook in every section, every melody derived from the hook's notes, a hook varied each time it
  returns, and a verse that reaches the top note. (practice)

## How Hooks Are Found, at Length

A note Claude wrote in another conversation, kept nearly whole, names and all. (practice throughout, but for the one finding marked measured)

Most hooks come from a small set of methods. The skill is less in having the idea than in generating lots of candidates and
recognizing the one that sticks.

### What Makes Something Stick

A hook is whatever part of a song cannot be shaken. Usually that is a melody or riff, but it can be a rhythm, a lyric phrase, a
sound or a chord move. The sticky ones tend to share a few traits:

- **Short.** One or two bars, a few seconds long, so it fits in the head at once.
- **Easy to sing.** A narrow range, often within a fifth or sixth, mostly stepwise with one leap.
- **Simple pitch, interesting rhythm.** Many hooks repeat one or two notes, and the rhythm does the work.
- **Repeated in the song**, but with enough variation that it does not wear out.
- **A familiar shape with one odd detail.** A 2017 study of earworms by Jakubowski and colleagues found that catchy tunes tend to have an ordinary overall shape,
  rising then falling, combined with unusual intervals or rhythms. The frame is predictable and one detail surprises. (measured)

### Methods Writers Use

- **Start from the words.** The Nashville approach. The hook is the title, and its melody is found by saying the phrase out loud and exaggerating its
  natural stresses and pitch until it becomes singing. Good prosody, where the stressed syllables land on strong beats and higher
  notes, makes a line feel inevitable.
- **Mumble over a loop.** The main modern pop method. A producer makes a track, often just four chords and a beat. A topliner (the
  person writing the vocal melody) sings nonsense syllables over it for dozens of passes, recording everything, then combs through the recording
  for the two seconds that work, and the words come later. John Seabrook's The Song Machine describes this workflow. Writers in
  the Max Martin school add "melodic math": phrases whose lengths and
  syllable counts mirror or answer each other.
- **Rhythm first.** Tap or clap a rhythm until it feels good, then add pitches. Hip-hop hooks often work this way: a chant-like
  line with few pitches, built on cadence and repetition.
- **Find it with the hands.** Guitar riffs often come from shapes that fall naturally under the fingers, like pentatonic boxes
  and open strings. Since the instrument suggests the idea, riffs found this way tend to sound idiomatic.
- **Statement, repeat, break.** Classical theory calls this the sentence: state a short idea, repeat it (often transposed), then
  break the pattern with a continuation that runs to a cadence. It is the shape of a huge number of pop choruses and of
  Beethoven's Fifth. It works because the repeat teaches the listener the pattern and the break rewards the
  learning.
- **Hold something back.** Choruses hit harder when the verse has saved something for them. The verse stays low, busy and
  syncopated, or avoids the tonic or the top note. The chorus hook then lifts into a higher register with longer notes on the
  beat, finally landing on the note the verse kept avoiding.
- **Transform something already liked.** Take the rhythm of one tune and the contour of another, then invert it, displace it by
  an eighth note, or move it to a new mode. (For llamatron this method is not used on any existing work: the rules about
  melodies forbid it.)

### How It Varies by Genre

- **Rock**: the riff is the hook, often instrumental and repeated through the whole song.
- **Electronic dance music**: the hook is often a sound: the drop's lead patch, a chopped vocal, a sidechained chord stab.
- **Jazz**: a head with one memorable motif that the soloists can quote and bend.
- **Film**: the leitmotif, sometimes as small as two notes, tied to a character or idea and transformed as the story changes.
- **Folk**: the refrain, built so a room can sing it back after one hearing.
- **Video games**: the hook has to survive hundreds of loops, so writers favor melodies that resolve back into their own beginning.

### Testing a Hook

Writers use a few blunt tests:

- Sing it with no accompaniment and see whether it still works.
- Ask whether a stranger could hum it back after hearing it twice.
- Check whether it is remembered the next morning without the recording.

If a hook needs its chords or production to sound good, it is probably an arrangement idea, not a hook.

### In llamatron

The mumble-over-a-loop method maps closely onto llamatron. Write the loop as a progression, then have a generator produce many
short candidate patterns under hook-style rules: a range of a sixth or less, at most one leap, ending on degree 1, 3 or 5, with
some syncopation. A seed browser would let a person audition them and keep the ones that stick. The machine does the mumbling
and the person picks the keepers, which is how many working writers already split the job.

## What Makes a Hook Good, and How One Is Chosen

- In order of weight: a rhythm that is known when clapped with no pitches, and unlike the verse's; three to seven notes in one or
  two bars, sung in one breath; a small cell said twice, or a note repeated, then one change; three or four pitches within about a
  sixth, with one leap at most; one surprise and only one, since two cancel; the long note a note of the chord on a strong beat or
  pushed just ahead of one; one highest note, once a statement, above anything in the verse; room to breathe after it. All of
  these can be held by rule. Whether the hook is loved is judged only by ear. (practice)
- So a program makes many hooks, discards each that breaks a rule, and lets a person hear the rest and choose: hearing and
  choosing comes first, nudges to the one chosen second (higher, lower, simpler, busier, a longer last note, more repetition,
  another ending), and the technical parts are what an expert opens afterwards. A few candidates at a time, unlike one another,
  each heard over the chorus's chords with the drums, never bare. (practice)
- A hook is not one mould: the long note may come first, the figure may fall, or sit on one note. What a person can say of a hook
  in plain words is its shape (rises, falls, up and back, stays put, jumps then steps back), where it starts against the beat,
  how many notes it has, how high it reaches, and whether its ending is settled or left hanging. (practice)
- A hook is commonly written to a title: one syllable a note, stressed syllables on strong beats or pushed just ahead, the main
  word on the long note, small leading words as a pickup, and silence after the phrase. (practice)
- By genre: an anthem's hook is a few long notes, high, on the beat, settled; in soul and funk the rhythm is the hook, short and
  syncopated with much silence, answered by the band; a tuneful pop hook goes up and back with one leap and repeats at once; a
  blues hook is a falling refrain line answered by an instrument, or a low riff; a dance track's hook is an instrumental cell of
  one or two bars and two or three pitches, looped unchanged. Who carries the hook, a voice or an instrument, is part of it. (practice)
- The hook is written first where the song is led by its singing, and everything else is written to make it land; where a song
  is led by a riff the riff is written first and the singing is written into its gaps. (practice)
