# Hooks

- A hook is whatever a listener carries away: usually a short phrase of melody with its words, but also a riff, a rhythm, a drum
  figure, a sound, or a stop. A song often has several: the chorus's sung hook, an instrumental hook heard in the intro and between
  sections, and a rhythmic hook in the groove. (practice)
- Tunes that stick tend to share three things: a quicker pace, an overall shape that is common and easy to follow (rising then
  falling, or the reverse), and inside that plain shape one or two unusual moves, a leap larger than expected or a repeated note
  where a step was expected. Ordinary enough to hold, odd enough to notice. (measured, as a tendency and not a recipe)
- Short is strong: a hook is commonly one or two bars, a handful of notes, with a rhythm that could be clapped and recognized. The
  rhythm identifies a hook at least as much as its pitches do. (practice)
- Repetition makes a hook, and exact repetition wears it out. The usual pattern is to state it, state it again, vary it (higher,
  or with a changed ending), and return: the shapes AABA and AAAB over four phrases. (practice)
- Placement: the sung hook at the start or the end of the chorus, on strong beats and long notes; the instrumental hook in the
  intro, after each chorus, and under the ending. Hearing the hook before the first verse makes the chorus feel familiar the first
  time it arrives. (practice)
- A hook needs room: the bars around it are plainer, other players thin out or double it, and the drums often mark its arrival.
  A second line fighting the hook weakens both. (practice)
- A hook should be singable: mostly steps and small leaps, within about an octave, sitting where an untrained voice is comfortable.
  (practice)

## Building a Sung Hook

- **A figure and its answer**: the hook proper is one bar or less, a few short notes that drive into one long note. The bar after
  answers it lower, with the same short notes in a looser order and a breath at the end. Figure and answer together make the
  line; the figure alone is what is remembered. (practice)
- **The long note is the point**: the figure's last note is its longest, at least half a bar, on a note of the chord, and it is
  the highest note of the song. A hook whose peak the verse has already used has nothing left to give. So the verse is written
  under a ceiling, and only the hook, a bridge or a pre-chorus's last climb may pass it. (practice)
- **Four ways into the peak**: one note repeated and then a leap up; a climb by step; the peak first, a fall away and a leap
  back; a turn about the two notes under it. The leap or the repeated note is the one odd move inside a plain shape. (practice)
- **Few pitches**: three or four in the figure. The verse may wander over more; the hook does not. (practice)
- **Across a chorus**: figure and answer, the same with the answer's last note risen to the peak or above, the first again, then
  a different closing line that comes down home. The rise in the second line is the lift; the plain third line is the return.
  Six bare repeats of a figure in eight bars wears it out; three, each answered, does not. (practice, and heard in trial tunes)
- **Length of notes is not the contrast**: measured on tunes made by rule, a hook's notes are not reliably longer on average than
  its verse's, since the short notes of the figure pull the average down. What tells verse from chorus is the height, the one
  long note, the fewer pitches and the exact return. (measured on generated tunes only)

## More Than One Hook

- A long or loosely played song wants several: the sung hook, an instrumental figure, and a returning theme that a jam leaves and
  finds again. Each must be short enough to be known after one hearing, and they should differ in rhythm before they differ in
  pitch. (practice)
