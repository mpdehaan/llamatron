# Rhythm and Groove

## The Drummer's Job

- Keep time, say where the song is, and make the others sound good. A drummer plays for the song: the commonest fault is too much,
  too many subdivisions, ghost notes or fills, above all when other players are busy. (practice)
- The kick steadies and the snare drives. The kick works with the bass, landing with the bass's main notes; the two are one
  instrument to the ear. (practice)

## The Groove

- The **backbeat**, the snare on beats two and four, is the spine of rock, pop, soul and funk. The plainest groove is kick on one
  and three, snare on two and four, the hi-hat in eighths. (practice)
- The **hi-hat or ride** sets how busy the song feels: quarters are heavy and open, eighths the default, sixteenths driving. Louder
  strokes on the beat and softer between give bounce; an even line is mechanical. Opening the hi-hat just before a beat lifts
  into it. (practice)
- **Kick variations** give a groove its name: extra kicks before or after the main ones push it; a kick on every beat (four on the
  floor) is dance music's pulse; kicks that dodge the beat make funk. **Half time** puts the backbeat on three alone and feels half
  as fast at the same tempo; **double time** the reverse. (practice)
- **Ghost notes** are very soft snare strokes on the sixteenths between the main hits, a tenth to a third as loud as the backbeat.
  They fill the gaps with motion and are felt more than heard. Funk, soul and shuffles live on them; punk and four-on-the-floor
  styles hardly use them. Too many clutter. (practice)
- **Swing and shuffle**: the off-beat lands late, at the third triplet. A shuffle plays that long-short pair all the time; straight
  time with a slight lean is a feel between. (practice)
- **Loudness is the instrument**: one hit at three loudnesses is three sounds. A real part has accents, ordinary strokes and
  ghosts, and no two bars quite alike. Playing just ahead of the beat sounds urgent, just behind sounds relaxed; a player keeps to
  one or the other, and the hi-hat may sit apart from the kick and snare. (practice)

## Fills

- A fill marks the end of a phrase and hands the song to the next. It belongs at the end of a phrase, never in its middle.
  (practice)
- Size follows the weight of the join: a small figure, or only a change in the hi-hat, at each four bars; a beat or two at eight;
  half a bar to a bar into a new section; the biggest into the last chorus. Most fills are a bar or less. (practice)
- A fill resolves: it lands on the first beat of the next bar with a crash and the kick together, and the groove returns at once.
  A fill that runs past that beat, or leaves the time unclear, fails. (practice)
- Fills build by adding notes, moving from snare to toms, high to low, and getting louder. A fill can also be less: a bar of
  silence or of kick alone marks a join as strongly. (practice)
- Lead-in fills set up an entrance: a beat or a bar before the band lands. Starting on beat three and resolving on one is common.
  (practice)

- A fill is to the form what a dominant chord is to the harmony: the fill is the tension and the crash and kick on the next
  first beat are the release. Fills too often, or all one size, flatten the order of sizes and the form stops reading. (practice)
- A fill is a cue before it is decoration: drum traditions with a lead drummer have set calls that the whole group answers
  with the change of section. (practice)
- Raising the subdivision raises the pull: eighths, then sixteenths, then sixteenth triplets; a fill that goes up a subdivision
  in its last beat pulls hardest at the landing. (practice)
- Groups against the bar: sixteenths in threes (two hands and a kick, say), four threes and a four filling a bar of 4/4. The
  threes fight the beat and the last group of four gives in to it, so the landing feels owed. Fives and sevens work the same
  way. (practice)
- A linear fill has no two limbs striking at once, so the fill sounds like one line passed around the kit, kick included.
  (practice)
- Texture without speed: a grace note before a stroke, a doubled stroke, a sticking pattern spread over the toms. (practice)
- A fill going up the kit, low to high, lifts where the usual high to low falls; it is the rarer kind. (practice)
- The beginner's fault is a fill that hurries, so that the landing comes early. A good fill grows out of the groove and lands
  back in it. (practice)

## The Kit by Section

- The same pattern on another surface is another section: closed hi-hat in a verse, the ride or an open hi-hat in a chorus, toms
  under a bridge or an intro, a rim click in a quiet verse. (practice)
- A crash marks the first beat of a section; a chorus may crash every two or four bars. (practice)
- Leaving out is arranging: no kick in a first verse, hi-hat alone in an intro, everything out for a beat before a chorus. (practice)
- A second verse is a little busier than the first, an added kick or more ghost notes, so that the song keeps growing.
  (practice)
- A pre-chorus builds, the snare on every beat, eighths on the low tom, a rise in loudness; or does the opposite and thins or
  stops, so that the chorus hits harder by contrast. (practice)
- A bridge is contrast: another groove, another cymbal (the ride's bell is a favourite), half time, a groove on the toms, or
  the drums out and built again from nothing. (practice)
- The last chorus is the biggest; often a chorus with the drums out comes first, the voice carrying, and then the full kit
  comes back with a crash on every bar. (practice)
- Ways a drummer ends a song: holding the groove through a fade; a vamp whose fills grow; one hit with the whole band; the
  big ending, cymbals washing and toms rolling over a held chord until the last hit; slowing down; or the intro's groove
  again, so that the song closes as it opened. (practice)
- The form as numbers: a level of energy for each section, say intro 3, verse 4, pre-chorus 6, chorus 8, verse 5, bridge 3,
  last chorus 10, and the drum part follows the curve. (practice)

## More on Sections, Balance and Fills

- **Change the surface, keep the groove**: the quickest way to say a new section has come is the cymbal, not more notes. A ladder
  of intensity: tight closed hi-hat (low), ride played on its bow (middle), a crash on the first beat and then the ride (high). The
  reverse, ride in the verse and an open hi-hat in the chorus, also works; what matters is that the surface changes. (practice)
- **Crashes**: on the first beat of a section, then about every four bars at a phrase's start in an open chorus; the hand returns
  to the time-keeping cymbal within a beat. Crashing all the time is no accent at all. A ride's bell is an accent to spend rarely,
  as on the first bar of a chorus. (practice)
- **Three faders**: the snare is the clearest voice, the kick supports and locks with the bass, the cymbals carry time under both
  and never over the singer. A quieter verse lowers the cymbals first and keeps the snare clear; a bigger chorus gives the snare
  and kick more weight and opens the cymbals without making them louder than the snare. (practice)
- **Hold a groove**: one groove for eight or sixteen bars, the same at the end as at the start. In a build, change one thing at a
  time: loudness, or how many notes, or the surface. (practice)
- **Kick and bass**: anchor the first beat, often the third, line up with any figure the bass repeats, and take out kicks that
  blur it; under a long bass note play fewer kicks. (practice)
- **Fill lengths, each landing on the next bar's first beat**: one beat (from beat four), two beats (from beat three), one bar.
  Shapes from plain to wide: snare alone, snare and one tom, toms going down. Eighths before sixteenths: a fill that drifts is too
  hard. The landing, crash and kick together, is louder and surer than the fill. The time keeps going through a fill, a foot on
  the hi-hat or one hand on the pulse, and a fill never shortens or lengthens its bar. (practice)

## Named Grooves

- **Soul's four-snare beat**: the snare on every beat with the kicks between, a tambourine on every beat or on two and four;
  driving and bright. (practice)
- **Second line**: a New Orleans street beat. A walking pulse of four with snare accents, buzzes and rolls bouncing around it, felt
  between straight and swung, often shaped by a clave of three hits then two; the root of that city's funk. (practice)
- **Punk beats**: the fast two-step, kick and snare alternating on every eighth like a sped-up polka; and a harder variant whose
  kicks push ahead of the beat for a rushed, tumbling feel, around two hundred beats a minute. (practice)
- **Shuffles**: the plain shuffle, close to a rock beat with the off-beat late; the Chicago kind leaning on two and four and the
  Texas kind on one and three; the half-time shuffle with the snare on three alone and ghost notes filling the triplets; the
  up-beat shuffle, accents on the last triplet of each beat over a kick on every beat; and the slow blues in twelve-eight.
  (practice)

## Rhythm Beyond the Drums

- **Syncopation**, accents where the beat is not, is what makes a part groove; a syncopated part needs a steady one beside it to be
  heard against. (practice)
- Parts lock by sharing some hits and avoiding others: a rhythm guitar that plays in the snare's gaps, a bass that leaves the
  backbeat alone. Everyone on every eighth is a wall, not a groove. (practice)

## Measured: Drum Kits by Style

From recordings of drummers playing each style to a click, as MIDI, kept on this computer only: how often each drum sounds on
each sixteenth of a bar, and what a fill is made of. Blues, country, gospel and punk are small samples. (measured)

- **Rock**: kick on 1 in two bars in three and on 3 in under half, with the and of 3 and beat 2 each in a third: the kick moves
  from bar to bar. Snare on 2 and 4 in seven bars in ten. Hi-hat eighths, or the ride on quarters. A third of snare notes are
  ghosts. Median 105 beats a minute.
- **Soul**: the plainest: kick on 1 (nine in ten) and the and of 3 (over half), snare on 2 and 4 nearly always, little else.
- **Funk**: kick on 1, then off the beat: the a of 1, the a of 2, the and of 3. Snare on 2 and 4 plus ghosts on the e and a
  (half of all snare notes are ghosts). Sixteenths on the hi-hat, an open hi-hat on the and of 3. Almost no toms.
- **Hip hop**: kick on 1, the and and a of 1, the and of 2, and 3; snare on 2 and 4; hats on eighths with sixteenths between.
- **Jazz**: the ride on every beat with the skip note late (58% of the way through the beat at medium tempos), the hi-hat foot
  on 2 and 4, kick light and scattered, snare comping evenly across the bar with half its notes ghosts.
- **Blues shuffle**: the off-beat falls two thirds through the beat, a true triplet; kick on 1 and 3 with a pickup before each;
  snare on 2 and 4 nearly always; six snare notes in ten are ghosts.
- **Country train beat**: kick on every beat, the snare brushing every sixteenth with accents on 2 and 4, hi-hat on the ands.
- **Reggae**: kick heaviest on 3 (the one drop) and absent from 1 in most bars; swung; open hi-hats on the ands.
- **Punk**: backbeat at double time (a snare every other eighth), kick on 1 and pushed around it, the floor tom or crash-ride
  carrying time more often than a closed hi-hat; the kick takes a fifth of a fill's notes.
- **Dance**: kick on all four with 1 and 3 surest, snare or clap on 2 and 4, open hi-hat on the off-beats, sixteenth hats.
- **Fills, every style**: a median just under one bar (3.3 to 3.8 beats), a quarter of them half a bar, a quarter longer than a
  bar in rock, soul and New Orleans styles. Density about four to five hits a beat, six in hip hop and New Orleans. Made of
  snare (four to five notes in ten), toms (a quarter to three in ten), kick (one in ten; two in punk and Latin), a crash almost
  never inside the fill. Funk fills are half snare; pop fills use the most toms.
- **Ghost notes** by style: country and blues six in ten snare notes, funk and jazz half, rock a third, punk and pop a quarter.
