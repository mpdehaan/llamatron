# Keyboards and Organs

Instruments are named as products; no player is. (practice throughout, from lessons and histories of the instruments)

## The Three Organs of Rock

- **The tonewheel organ with a rotating speaker** (the Hammond kind): thick and warm, shaped by drawbars that mix harmonics, a
  percussive click at the start of a note, and a speaker that spins slow (a shimmer) or fast (a wobble). It has no touch
  response, so dynamics come from the drawbars, the swell pedal, how many notes are held and the speaker's speed. Overdriven it
  growls like a guitar.
- **The combo organs** (the Vox and Farfisa kind): transistor, portable, thin, bright and reedy; the first a little rounder, the
  second raspier and more aggressive. No rotating speaker; a plain vibrato. The sound of garage rock, early psychedelia and,
  later, new wave. Cheap, cutting and unashamed.
- The same notes on each say a different decade and scene, as with drum machines.

## What an Organist Plays

- **Pads**: a held chord of few notes spread over an octave, under everything; the speaker slow in verses and switched to fast
  for a chorus or a solo, the change itself a lift.
- **Stabs**: short chords on the off-beats or with the snare; in garage rock a bed of staccato chords can be the song's hook in
  place of a tune, over two chords. Staccato organ playing owes something to two-part keyboard inventions of the baroque.
- **Riffs**: a distorted organ riff played with both hands is as heavy as a guitar riff and can be the song's main hook.
- **Fills and answers**: grace notes, chromatic slides into a chord, a walk down between chords, pitchless percussive pops, a
  glissando up to or down from a stab, smashing the keys for a swell or a growl.
- **Voicings** are small. Big two-handed piano chords turn to mud on an organ; two to four notes do the job.
- **Bass from the keyboard**: with no bass player the left hand plays the bass on a separate keyboard bass or the organ's lower
  manual: simple figures repeated without change, in the manner of boogie-woogie piano, often just the root in eighths, while
  the right hand plays chords, thirds and lines. Such a bass is steadier and plainer than a bass guitarist's and locks with the
  drums as one machine; it is why some bands sound hypnotic.
- **Solos** borrow from jazz organ and from modes: long lines over a two-chord vamp.

## Organ and Guitar Together

- The organ holds the harmony so the guitar need not: a guitarist over an organ pad plays single lines and leaves chords alone.
- In Latin rock a two-chord organ vamp, minor with the raised sixth of the Dorian mode, sits under congas and timbales playing
  interlocking patterns, the timbales leading; the whole band states the vamp, drops back, and the guitar sings a slow tune over
  it with long sustained notes, then solos at length. Organ fills answer the guitar.
- Organ and guitar trade the answering role by section, and double a riff for weight.

## The Organ as the Song

- **The descending bass under an organ tune**: a bass line walking down the scale one step at a bar or half bar, slow, with a
  free, singing organ melody above it, borrowed from baroque ground basses. Organ and piano together, the piano playing the
  chords and the organ the tune, with a plain slow drum beat. The organ's tune is the hook, stated before the voice and between
  verses. A slow tempo, a major key, a hymn's dignity.
- **In classic rock and jam music** the organ is the backdrop for the lead guitar: pads and rhythmic comping under the solo,
  swelling as the solo rises, the speaker switched to fast at the peak, then a solo of its own. With two guitars the organ takes
  the middle and leaves the low and high registers to them.
- **The roots-rock organist** takes cues from the singer: pads and swells that follow the words, never a part for its own sake.
- **The rock pianist-songwriter**: a pounding chord riff on piano as the song's hook, played like a rhythm guitar; a classical
  introduction echoed at the end.

## Keyboards in a Band, at Length

throughout)

Keyboards are the most powerful accompanying instrument in a band. Two hands can cover bass, harmony, melody and rhythm at once.
That makes the main skill restraint: choosing which job to take in this song and leaving the rest to the other players.

### Staying Out of the Way

The first rule of band keyboard playing is to manage register. With a bassist present, the left hand stays out of the low end, or
it doubles the bass on purpose instead of by accident. With a guitarist, the two split the midrange, often with one comping while
the other holds sustained chords (pads). Around a singer, the keyboard plays in the gaps between vocal lines, the way a guitarist
plays fills. A keyboardist who plays full two-handed piano in a five-piece band muddies everything.

### Voicings

How a chord is stacked decides its sound more than which chord it is.

- **Pop and rock**: right-hand triads, often inverted so they move smoothly, over a left-hand root, octave, or root and fifth.
  Common tones are held and other voices move by step.
- **Shell voicings**: root, 3rd and 7th only. The lean sound of bebop piano and stride.
- **Rootless voicings**: the Bill Evans and Wynton Kelly approach. When the bassist covers the root, the left hand plays 3-5-7-9 or 7-9-3-5. They sound rich and leave the
  bass alone.
- **Quartal voicings**: chords built in fourths, as McCoy Tyner played them, or the "So What" voicing: three fourths with a
  major third on top. Open and modern, with no strong pull
  toward a key.
- **Upper structures**: a triad on top of a tritone. A D major triad over the 3rd and 7th of C7 gives the 9, sharp 11 and 13 in
  one easy shape.
- **Gospel**: passing chords between almost every pair of chords. Chromatic walk-ups, diminished chords connecting diatonic ones,
  tritone substitutions and sus chords make the harmony feel constantly in motion.

### Accompaniment Styles by Genre

The rhythm of the comping identifies the genre faster than the harmony does.

- **Classical**: Alberti bass (low, high, middle, high), broken-chord arpeggios, and the waltz's "oom-pah-pah". Chopin's nocturnes
  spread the left hand wider than an octave.
- **Stride and ragtime**: bass note on beats 1 and 3, chord on 2 and 4. The left hand leaps all evening.
- **Boogie-woogie**: a driving left-hand eighth-note figure walking through the chord, with the right hand playing riffs on top.
- **1950s rock and doo-wop ballads**: pounding eighth-note chords, or right-hand triplet chords in 12/8. Little Richard and
  Fats Domino are the models.
- **Jazz comping**: short, irregular, syncopated stabs that react to the soloist. "Comp" comes from complement. Red Garland's Charleston
  rhythm (one beat, then the "and" of 2) is the starting point. Space is a large part of it.
- **Country**: Floyd Cramer's "slip note", a grace note a whole step below that slides into the chord tone. A piano's imitation of a steel
  guitar bend.
- **Reggae**: piano and organ chops on the offbeats, plus the organ "bubble", a bouncing pattern that fills the space between the
  skanks.
- **Funk**: clavinet played like a funk rhythm guitar. Muted, percussive, interlocking sixteenths, as in Stevie Wonder's work.
- **Salsa**: the piano montuno, a syncopated, octave-doubled arpeggio pattern locked to the clave.
- **New Orleans**: Professor Longhair's mix of rhumba and boogie, with Caribbean rhythm in the left hand.
- **House**: syncopated stabs on minor 7th and major 7th chords. The piano becomes a drum.

### Organ Is a Different Instrument

A tonewheel organ shares the keyboard but almost nothing else with the piano. It has no velocity and no decay: a note sounds at
full volume until the finger lifts. That changes how it is played.

- Dynamics come from the expression pedal, which swells and fades held chords.
- Articulation is in the release. Organists phrase by lifting fingers. A short release gives chops and stabs, a held one gives a pad.
- Drawbars are orchestration. Registration, the mix of harmonics pulled out, sets the tone of each section. The first three
  drawbars pulled out fully (the "888" setting) is the classic gospel and jazz sound. The percussion tab gives a click on each
  attack.
- The rotating speaker's speed switch is a structural move. Switching from slow to fast rotation builds intensity going into a
  chorus or the peak of a solo. Switching back cools it down.
- Organ as glue. In rock, sustained organ chords under the guitars fill the mix without anyone noticing, until it is gone. Jon Lord
  pushed the organ through distortion so it could play rhythm guitar.
- Gospel organ answers the preacher and the choir, filling every gap in a conversation of call and response.
- Organ trios have no bassist. Jimmy Smith walked the bass line with his left hand or his feet while soloing with his right.

### Soloing

Piano soloing falls into a few approaches:

- **Horn-style lines**: a right-hand bebop line with sparse left-hand comping underneath. The Bud Powell model.
- **Block chords**: George Shearing's "locked hands" doubles the melody in octaves with a chord in between, so the whole band seems to move as
  one. Red Garland and Oscar Peterson used octaves and block chords for climaxes.
- **Faking blue notes**: a piano cannot bend, so blues players crush the flat 3 into the 3, playing both at once or one as a
  grace note into the other. The same idea as the guitar's bend from flat 3 toward 3, done with two keys.
- **Tremolos and rolls**, which give a piano the sustain it lacks. Gospel and blues pianists lean on them at the peak.
- **Glissandi and pounding chords**: Jerry Lee Lewis.
- **Pentatonics in fourths and side-slipping**: McCoy Tyner's approach to modal jazz.

Organ soloing uses the instrument's sustain. Holding a high note while the rotating speaker spins fast is one of the biggest
sounds in music. Organists also hold one note while trilling or grinding against it, use palm smears and glissandi, and play fast
bebop lines with the percussion tab giving each note a pluck.

Synth soloing is where keyboards get bends and vibrato. Pitch bend and the mod wheel let Jan Hammer phrase like a guitarist, and
the Minimoog leads of Keith Emerson, Rick Wakeman and Herbie Hancock depend on glide and legato.

The structure of a keyboard solo follows the same arc as a guitar solo (`LEADS.md`): start sparse, build in register and density,
peak, and resolve. On organ the speed switch of the rotating speaker often marks the climax.
