# Song Structure

## Sections

- A song is a few kinds of section, each returning. The **verse** carries the story: its words change each time, its tune stays. The
  **chorus** carries the song's main idea and its hook: words and tune both return, and it is the high point of energy. The
  **bridge** (middle eight) comes once, late, and exists to be different, so that the last chorus feels like coming home. A
  **pre-chorus** is a short ramp between a verse and a chorus, useful when the two are far apart in range or energy. A **solo** takes
  a verse's or a chorus's chords with an instrument in the singer's place. An **intro** and an **ending** frame the rest. (practice)
- Older forms have no chorus: the **twelve-bar blues** repeats one chorus of twelve bars throughout, and the **thirty-two bar form**
  is two like phrases, a contrasting middle and the first phrase again, the title in the first or last line. (practice)
- Sections are nearly always 4, 8, 12 or 16 bars, built from phrases of 2 or 4. An odd length is a deliberate surprise: a bar added
  before a chorus to make the listener wait, or a bar dropped to arrive early. (practice)

## Contrast Between Sections

- A chorus stands out from its verse by several of these at once: a higher range, longer notes, fewer and more repeated words, a
  simpler tune made of short repeated phrases, fuller instruments, a busier or more open drum part, and often a move to chords the
  verse held back. A verse may sit low and wordy on few pitches so that the chorus's leap is felt. (practice)
- The title usually sits at the start or the end of the chorus, on its longest or highest notes. (practice)
- Energy over a whole song usually rises in steps: the second verse a little fuller than the first, the last chorus the fullest,
  with a drop somewhere, often the bridge or a stripped-down verse, so that the last rise has somewhere to come from. (practice)

## The Pre-Chorus

- **What it does**: a ramp of 2, 4 or sometimes 8 bars between verse and chorus that makes the chorus feel arrived at. It returns
  with the same tune each time, and often the same words, so it is a second small hook. (practice)
- **Chords**: it leaves the verse's loop and avoids the home chord, starting on IV, ii or vi, the chords changing faster than in
  the verse (two a bar where the verse had one), and ends on V or IV held, wanting home, which the chorus's first chord gives.
  Where the verse and chorus share one loop, the pre-chorus is the only place the harmony moves. (practice)
- **Tune**: it climbs: each phrase starts a step higher, or the phrases shorten, so the chorus's first note is the top. (practice)
- **Band**: the drums build (kick on every beat, the snare doubling to eighths then sixteenths in the last bar, or a drop to
  half-time and then the fill); the bass climbs or pedals on V; guitars open from muted to ringing; a bar of near silence or one
  held chord before the chorus is as strong as a fill. (practice)
- **When**: rare in the 1960s (about one chart song in fourteen), one in four by the 1980s, and usual in later pop, pop punk and
  polished hard rock. Roots styles, blues and country seldom have one: there the verse's last line turns around instead. (measured)
- The first verse may go to the chorus by way of it, or the first pass may hold it back so that it is new the second time.

## Shapes of Whole Songs

- **Verses in a row** (blues, rock and roll, rockabilly, folk): intro, three to five verses with an instrumental break after the
  second or third, the title as each verse's last line (a refrain), and a tag. No chorus; variety comes from the break and the band.
- **Verse and chorus alternating** (roots rock, country, soul, jam): verse, chorus, verse, chorus, a break over the verse's chords,
  chorus; a bridge in a quarter of songs or fewer. Country often tells two verses before the first chorus.
- **Thirty-two bars with a middle** (early beat groups, standards, doo-wop): two verses ending on the title, a contrasting middle
  eight starting away from home, a last verse; then the middle and last verse again, sometimes with half a verse as a solo.
- **Verse, chorus, verse, chorus, bridge, chorus** (later rock, pop punk, new wave, radio rock): the commonest shape from the 1980s
  on; the bridge drops down or changes key color, and the last chorus comes twice, the first time stripped or the second time
  with a lift.
- **The same with a solo** (hard rock, arena rock): the solo over the verse's or chorus's chords after the second chorus, before or
  in place of the bridge, then a double chorus.
- **Chorus first** (soul, gospel, reggae, some pop): the hook opens the song, then the verse explains it.
- **Groove forms** (funk, dance): one vamp throughout, sections made by who plays, with one bridge that changes the chord.
- A song grows longer with the decades: 8 sections in the 1960s, 9 in the 1970s, 11 in the 1980s, and the first chorus arrives
  later (bar 16, 20, 24). The second verse is often half the first's length in later styles, to reach the chorus sooner.

## How Songs Start

- An intro sets tempo, key and mood before the singer. Its length has fallen over time: hits of the mid-1980s averaged over twenty
  seconds of intro, and hits thirty years later about five. (measured) So eight bars suits an older style and two a modern one.
- The common ways: a **drum fill** of a beat to a bar into the whole band; the **drums alone** on the groove for one to four bars;
  a **count** on the sticks; **one instrument's riff** alone, then the band; **layers**, players joining one at a time at an even
  spacing; the **full band** on the first beat; and **straight in** on the verse or the chorus. (practice)
- A band lead-in tends to have stages: a setup by one thing alone, then the band landing, often with a crash and the hook on a lead
  instrument over the verse's chords, then the verse, where the singer's entrance is the event. (practice)
- Order of entrance: in groove music (funk, disco, soul, dance) drums, then bass, then chords, then a lead line, then the voice; in
  riff music (hard and arena rock, new wave) the riff first, then a fill, then drums and bass together. Bass and drums enter
  together or one stage apart, and the voice last. (practice)
- Dance music adds a part every eight bars and keeps sixteen or more bars of drums at the top, so that one record can be mixed into
  the next. (practice)
- A start with nothing to set it up, a chord instrument sounding on the first instant, is the least natural on a recording unless
  the style is meant to feel abrupt. (judgment)

- **Lead-ins by style**: rock and roll and rockabilly, a two- or four-bar riff or a pickup into the verse, sometimes the voice
  alone for a beat; beat groups, a four-bar riff or the chorus's last line played; folk rock, the chiming figure alone, then the
  band; hard rock, the riff alone twice, a fill, the riff with the band twice (8 bars, often 16 in the 1980s); punk and pop punk,
  a count and straight in, or four bars of guitar alone then all; country, a kickoff by fiddle, steel or guitar over the last
  line's chords (the turnaround), 2 to 4 bars; bluegrass, a kickoff of a full verse by one instrument; blues, the last four bars
  of the form as an intro, starting on V; soul, a horn line or a drum pickup into the groove; funk, the drums or the guitar's
  figure alone, then one part at a time; yacht rock, the keyboard's chords with a short lead line; ballads, piano or guitar alone.
- **The join into each section** matters as much as the top: a fill or a pickup into every chorus, a crash on its first beat, a
  stop of a beat or a bar before it; a drop to fewer players at each verse so the next chorus has room to rise. (practice)

## How Songs End

- The **fade** over a repeating chorus ruled recordings from the 1950s into the 1980s and has nearly vanished from hits since, giving
  way to endings that stop. (measured)
- Endings a band can play: a **held chord** with a crash; a **hard stop** on the beat after the last bar; a **big finish**, the chord
  held while the drums roll and then one last hit together; a **tag**, the chorus's last line two or three times; a **slowing** of
  the last bars into a held chord; **stripping down**, players leaving one at a time; the **opening riff alone** once more; the
  blues **turnaround** walked down to a held chord. (practice)
- **Play-outs by style**: rock and roll and blues, the turnaround and a held chord, or the last line tagged three times; beat
  groups, a tag and a held chord with harmony, or a short fade; sixties to eighties pop, soul and yacht rock, a fade over the
  chorus or a vamp with the singer ad-libbing, its outro growing from 8 bars to 16; hard and arena rock, the big finish, or a
  fade over the solo guitar; southern and jam styles, a long instrumental play-out that doubles the tempo or stacks solos; punk
  and pop punk, a hard stop, or the last chord left ringing; country, the last line tagged and a ritard into a held chord;
  bluegrass, a tag and the standard closing lick; funk, the vamp to a fade or a stop on a hit together; dance, layers out.
- **The vamp out**: two or four bars of the chorus's or a new loop repeated, the band adding or the singer answering, is the usual
  body of a fade and of a long play-out. A fade needs something still changing in it, or it sounds like waiting. (practice)
- An ending mirrors a start: layers in, layers out; riff first, riff last. An ending that stops needs its tension resolved before
  the last note, or the silence sounds like a cut. (practice)

## Measured: Chart Songs by Decade

From the chord and section transcriptions of about 740 chart singles, 1958 to 1991, kept on this computer only; all genres of the
chart together, so a tendency of popular song, not of one style. (measured)

- **Intros**: nine songs in ten have one. Median 4 bars in the 1960s, 5 in the 1970s, 8 in the 1980s. The usual lengths are 4 and
  8, then 2; 16-bar intros appear in the 1970s and are common in the 1980s. No intro at all: about one song in ten.
- **Verse before chorus** in more than eight songs in ten; a chorus first in about 19% (1960s) falling to 8% (1980s).
- **Bars before the first chorus**: median 16 (1960s), 20 (1970s), 24 (1980s). Songs take longer to arrive as decades pass.
- **Lengths**: a verse's median is 12 bars in every decade (8, 12 and 16 all common); a chorus 8; a bridge 8; a solo 8 to 9.
- **What a song has**: a chorus in 70% (1960s), 85% (1970s), 94% (1980s); a bridge in 30 to 45%; an instrumental solo in 28%
  (1960s), 35% (1970s), 45% (1980s); a pre-chorus in 7%, 12%, 23%; an interlude in 21%, 28%, 37%.
- **Endings**: a fade in more than half of all songs, 56% (1960s) to 69% (1980s). An outro section in about four in ten, its
  median 8 bars (1960s), 12 (1970s), 16 (1980s). Ending cold on a chorus or verse is the minority, commonest early.
- **Size**: median 80 bars and 8 sections (1960s), 94 and 9 (1970s), 112 and 11 (1980s).

## Measured: Structure by Genre

Same dataset and cautions as "Measured: Chords by Genre" in `HARMONY.md`. Its section labels are intro, verse, chorus, bridge,
solo, instrumental, interlude and outro; it has no pre-chorus label, so that share is known only from the chart corpus. (measured)

- **Verse-only songs**: a chorus is labeled in only four songs in ten in traditional blues, under six in ten in rock and roll,
  rockabilly, doo-wop and jazz standards; seven in ten in classic rock; over nine in ten in polished eighties hard rock, pop
  punk, later radio rock and contemporary country.
- **Solos** are a hard rock habit: half of polished eighties hard rock, a third of hard rock and electric blues, a quarter of
  southern rock, a fifth of classic rock, an eighth of new wave, one in twenty in pop punk and emo, almost none in soul.
- **Bridges** rise with time and polish: six in ten pop punk songs, over half of later radio rock and contemporary country,
  four in ten in soul and new wave, a quarter in roots styles, a sixth in outlaw country and blues, an eighth in bluegrass.
- **Instrumental breaks** in place of solos: a third of bluegrass and ska songs.
- **Chorus first**: four in ten reggae songs, a fifth of gospel, a sixth of soul and bluegrass, under a tenth in rock.
- **Commonest whole shapes**: pop punk and later radio rock are intro, verse, chorus, verse, chorus, bridge, chorus more than
  any other style has one shape. Hard rock is the same with a solo before the last chorus. Rock and roll, rockabilly and blues
  are verses in a row with a break. Jam and southern styles alternate verse and chorus three times and then play.
- **Size**: roots and country songs have six sections and about fifty to sixty chord changes; hard rock eight and over ninety.
- **Intros** are labeled in six to eight songs in ten; least in outlaw country and gospel.

## How Intros Begin and Exit, at Length

### How Intros Begin

Rock and pop intros usually run four or eight bars and come in a handful of standard types:

- **The riff or hook alone.** One instrument states the song's signature idea, and the others enter in layers: guitar, then
  hi-hat, then full drums and bass. "Smoke on the Water" is the textbook case.
- **The chorus without the vocal.** The band plays the chorus chords, often with the hook on an instrument, so the chorus feels
  familiar when it arrives.
- **A verse vamp.** Two or four bars of the verse groove, waiting for the singer.
- **A drum intro.** A count-in, a pickup fill or a signature beat.
- **An atmospheric build.** A pad, noise or a filtered version of the track that swells into the song.
- **A cold open.** The vocal starts immediately, sometimes with the chorus itself.

Intros have shrunk a lot in the streaming era. A study by Hubert Léveillé Gauvin found that the average intro of hit songs fell from over 20 seconds in the
mid-1980s to about 5 by 2015, because listeners can skip. (measured)

### How Intros Exit

The handoff into the first verse is usually one of these:

- **Fill and crash.** A drum fill sets up the verse downbeat.
- **The drop-down.** The band thins out as the vocal enters, often to just bass and drums. The intro promises the energy of the
  chorus and the verse pulls back from it.
- **A stop.** The band hits a chord and leaves a beat of silence, and the singer's pickup notes lead into the verse over the gap.
- **A build.** A crescendo or a held dominant chord resolves on the verse's first beat.
- **No exit at all.** In riff-based songs, the riff carries on under the verse.

Intro material tends to return later. It often comes back as a short turnaround after the first chorus, and again as the outro to
bookend the song.

The exits from the song itself mirror these. The standard ones are the fade, the hard stop on a final hit, the tag (the last
line repeated three times), the ritardando, and the last chord left to ring.

## Section Lengths, Bridges and Transitions, at Length

### Typical Lengths

Nearly everything in rock and pop comes in multiples of four bars, and most sections are 8 or 16. These are typical lengths in 4/4:

| Section | Typical length | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intro | 4 or 8 bars | Often 2 or none in current pop |
| Verse | 8 or 16 | 12 in blues-based songs |
| Pre-chorus | 4 or 8 | |
| Chorus | 8 or 16 | Often an 8-bar idea played twice |
| Post-chorus | 4 or 8 | An instrumental or chanted hook |
| Turnaround | 2 or 4 | A short reset between chorus and next verse |
| Bridge | 8 | Hence the British name "middle eight"; sometimes 4 or 16 |
| Solo | 8 or 16 | 12 over a blues form |
| Breakdown | 4 or 8 | |
| Outro | 4 or 8, or a fade | |

### Solos

A solo usually borrows a section's chords, so it inherits that section's length. Most are played over the verse or chorus
progression. Three sizes cover most cases:

- 8 bars: a half-verse solo, common in pop and radio edits.
- 16 bars: the standard rock solo, one full verse or chorus.
- 12 or 24 bars: one or two choruses of a blues.

Classic rock stretches well past this, especially at the end of a song. Closing solos like the ones in "Comfortably Numb" and
"Free Bird" run for dozens of bars, and live versions go longer. Current pop tends the other way: often there is no solo, or the
slot goes to a 4- or 8-bar instrumental hook.

### Thinking in Seconds

Tempo changes what a bar count means, so it helps to convert. At 120 BPM there are 30 bars per minute, so 8 bars is 16 seconds
and a 3:30 song is about 105 bars. Most sections last 15 to 30 seconds whatever the tempo, which is why fast songs use 16-bar
sections where slow ballads use 8.

### Exceptions

Writers often bend the grid on purpose:

- **Extensions**: an extra bar or two at the end of a section to delay the chorus.
- **Cut bars**: a bar of 2/4, or a dropped bar, so the next section arrives early.
- **Odd phrases**: the verse of "Yesterday" is seven bars long.
- **Short second verses**: the second verse is often half the length of the first, to reach the chorus sooner.

These deviations work for the same reason as other broken rules (`PLAYER_STYLE.md`): the four-bar habit is strong enough that a
listener feels the extra or missing bar.

### Bridges

A bridge is designed in opposition to the rest of the song: whatever the verse and chorus have used most, the bridge uses least.
Transitions run on three levers: a signal that change is coming, a shift in energy, and a harmonic pull from an unstable chord to
a stable one.

The bridge usually comes once, after the second chorus, and its job is to make the final chorus sound fresh. Writers look at what
the song has done so far and move away from it:

- **Harmony.** If the verse and chorus start on the tonic, the bridge starts somewhere else, most often on IV, vi or ii. A
  major-key song often leans toward its relative minor here. Borrowed chords like flat VI and flat VII, or a brief change of key,
  are common.
- **Harmonic rhythm.** A song built on a fast four-chord loop might hold one chord for the whole bridge. A song with slow, static
  harmony might start changing chords twice a bar.
- **Melody.** The bridge takes a register the chorus has not used, and changes the phrasing. If earlier melodies start on the
  downbeat, the bridge starts off the beat. Short busy phrases give way to long notes, or the reverse.
- **Groove.** Half-time is the classic move. Others are double-time, stop-time hits, or dropping the drums entirely.
- **Texture.** A loud song strips down to voice and one instrument. A sparse song may add strings or stacked vocals.
- **Lyrics.** The bridge gives a new angle: a twist, a different point in time, or the wider view.

The type of song shapes the choice: loop-based pop often keeps the same chords and changes only the production (the "breakdown
bridge"); rock often uses a new riff, a new key, or the guitar solo as the bridge; EDM uses the breakdown before the final drop;
hip-hop may switch the beat.

### Verse Into Chorus: The Lift

- The pre-chorus builds. The melody rises, the chords change faster, and the section ends on an unstable chord, usually V or IV,
  that the chorus resolves.
- Drums signal it. The usual signals are a snare build, a fill and a crash on the chorus downbeat.
- The drop-out. The band cuts out for the last beat or bar, leaving silence or the vocal alone, and the chorus then enters at full
  strength. Taking energy away just before adding it is one of the most reliable tricks in arranging.
- The vocal pickup. The first words of the chorus begin before the bar line.
- The arrangement widens. The melody jumps up in register, and backing vocals, doubled guitars and open hi-hats come in.

### Chorus Into Verse: The Come-Down

- A turnaround. Two or four bars of the intro riff reset the song.
- A hard drop. The band crashes on the verse downbeat and thins out at once.
- A ring-over. The last chorus chord sustains into the verse's first bar.
- A partial retreat. The second verse keeps something from the chorus, such as extra percussion or a counter-line, so the energy
  does not fall all the way back to verse one.

### Into the Bridge

- A direct cut. The chorus ends and the bridge starts on a new chord with a new texture. The abruptness is deliberate.
- A pivot. The chorus's final tonic chord is reinterpreted, for example as V of IV, leading smoothly into a bridge that starts on
  IV. Or a deceptive move to vi opens the bridge in the relative minor.
- A drop-out. Everything cuts but a pad or one instrument.
- A fill into half-time.

### Out of the Bridge

This is usually the biggest transition in the song.

- Dominant preparation. The bridge ends on V, often held for a bar or two with a crescendo, a snare roll and a rising line.
  Classical theory calls this the retransition.
- Layered rebuild. After a breakdown, instruments re-enter one at a time, ending with the song's largest fill.
- Stop and slam. A beat of silence, then the full chorus.
- The breakdown chorus. The bridge leads into a quiet chorus with voice and minimal backing, and then the full band enters for
  the repeat.
- The key change. The bridge ends on V of a key a half step or whole step higher, and the last chorus arrives in the new key.

### Into the Ending

- The final chorus is doubled, with ad-libs, higher harmonies or a new counter-melody on the repeat.
- The tag. The last line repeats two or three times. Often a deceptive cadence (V to vi) delays the final tonic, so the true
  ending feels earned.
- An outro vamp. The hook or intro riff loops, under a fade or under solos.
- A bookend. The intro returns to close the song.
- A hard ending. The band plays a final figure together and stops, or holds the last chord while the cymbals swell. Sometimes it
  slows down first.
- A cold stop on the last word.
- A coda with new material, as in "Hey Jude" and "Layla".

### Intro Into the First Verse

The exits are above ("How Intros Begin and Exit, at Length"): fill and crash, the drop-down, the stop with a vocal pickup, and
the build. Two further points:

- **Carry-over.** Part of the intro usually continues, such as the riff, the drum groove or a pad, so the verse sounds like the
  same song.
- **Headroom.** The first verse is normally the sparsest full section. Starting small leaves room for every later section to grow.

## What Makes a Chorus Good

- A chorus differs from its verse on at least three counts: register, its centre a third to a fifth up; longer notes; fewer
  notes; a plainer, more repeated rhythm; starting on or just before the downbeat where the verse started after it. (practice)
- The hook is at an edge of the chorus, never inside a line: at the start of the first line or the end of the last, best both.
  A chorus that closes on something other than the hook gives up the place a listener remembers. (practice)
- The arrival is written: in the bar before, the band thins or stops, a fill, the harmony on the chord that pulls home, the
  melody rising or silent; then the full band on the first beat. The chorus ends with the hook on the home note, held, and a bar
  of space. It is as long as the verse or half of it, never longer the first time; the last may double. (practice)
- The band lifts a chorus by adding one or two things, not everything: open cymbals, a firmer bass, a harmony voice on the hook
  alone, a held pad or doubled guitar. How much a chorus lifts is one thing to a listener, moving register, note length,
  density and the band together, and it means something else in each genre: in a dance track the lift is the arrangement. (practice)
- A chorus fails when it does not lift, is too like the verse, is too busy, buries its hook, has no arrival or overstays; each
  can be measured and a failed one written again. (practice)
