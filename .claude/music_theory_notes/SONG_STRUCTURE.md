# Song Structure

## Sections

- A song is a few kinds of section, each returning. The **verse** carries the story: its words change each time, its tune stays. The
  **chorus** carries the song's main idea and its hook: words and tune both return, and it is the high point of energy. The
  **bridge** (middle eight) comes once, late, and exists to be different, so that the last chorus feels like coming home. A
  **pre-chorus** is a short ramp between a verse and a chorus, useful when the two are far apart in range or energy. A **solo** takes
  a verse's or a chorus's chords with an instrument in the singer's place. An **intro** and an **ending** frame the rest. (practice)
- Older forms have no chorus: the **twelve-bar blues** repeats one chorus of twelve bars throughout, and the **thirty-two bar form**
  is two like phrases, a contrasting middle and the first phrase again, the title in the first or last line. (practice)
- Sections are nearly always 4, 8, 12 or 16 bars, built from phrases of 2 or 4. An odd length is a deliberate surprise: a bar added
  before a chorus to make the listener wait, or a bar dropped to arrive early. (practice)

## Contrast Between Sections

- A chorus stands out from its verse by several of these at once: a higher range, longer notes, fewer and more repeated words, a
  simpler tune made of short repeated phrases, fuller instruments, a busier or more open drum part, and often a move to chords the
  verse held back. A verse may sit low and wordy on few pitches so that the chorus's leap is felt. (practice)
- The title usually sits at the start or the end of the chorus, on its longest or highest notes. (practice)
- Energy over a whole song usually rises in steps: the second verse a little fuller than the first, the last chorus the fullest,
  with a drop somewhere, often the bridge or a stripped-down verse, so that the last rise has somewhere to come from. (practice)

## How Songs Start

- An intro sets tempo, key and mood before the singer. Its length has fallen over time: hits of the mid-1980s averaged over twenty
  seconds of intro, and hits thirty years later about five. (measured) So eight bars suits an older style and two a modern one.
- The common ways: a **drum fill** of a beat to a bar into the whole band; the **drums alone** on the groove for one to four bars;
  a **count** on the sticks; **one instrument's riff** alone, then the band; **layers**, players joining one at a time at an even
  spacing; the **full band** on the first beat; and **straight in** on the verse or the chorus. (practice)
- A band lead-in tends to have stages: a setup by one thing alone, then the band landing, often with a crash and the hook on a lead
  instrument over the verse's chords, then the verse, where the singer's entrance is the event. (practice)
- Order of entrance: in groove music (funk, disco, soul, dance) drums, then bass, then chords, then a lead line, then the voice; in
  riff music (hard and arena rock, new wave) the riff first, then a fill, then drums and bass together. Bass and drums enter
  together or one stage apart, and the voice last. (practice)
- Dance music adds a part every eight bars and keeps sixteen or more bars of drums at the top, so that one record can be mixed into
  the next. (practice)
- A start with nothing to set it up, a chord instrument sounding on the first instant, is the least natural on a recording unless
  the style is meant to feel abrupt. (judgment)

## How Songs End

- The **fade** over a repeating chorus ruled recordings from the 1950s into the 1980s and has nearly vanished from hits since, giving
  way to endings that stop. (measured)
- Endings a band can play: a **held chord** with a crash; a **hard stop** on the beat after the last bar; a **big finish**, the chord
  held while the drums roll and then one last hit together; a **tag**, the chorus's last line two or three times; a **slowing** of
  the last bars into a held chord; **stripping down**, players leaving one at a time; the **opening riff alone** once more; the
  blues **turnaround** walked down to a held chord. (practice)
- An ending mirrors a start: layers in, layers out; riff first, riff last. An ending that stops needs its tension resolved before
  the last note, or the silence sounds like a cut. (practice)

## Measured: Chart Songs by Decade

From the chord and section transcriptions of about 740 chart singles, 1958 to 1991, kept on this computer only; all genres of the
chart together, so a tendency of popular song, not of one style. (measured)

- **Intros**: nine songs in ten have one. Median 4 bars in the 1960s, 5 in the 1970s, 8 in the 1980s. The usual lengths are 4 and
  8, then 2; 16-bar intros appear in the 1970s and are common in the 1980s. No intro at all: about one song in ten.
- **Verse before chorus** in more than eight songs in ten; a chorus first in about 19% (1960s) falling to 8% (1980s).
- **Bars before the first chorus**: median 16 (1960s), 20 (1970s), 24 (1980s). Songs take longer to arrive as decades pass.
- **Lengths**: a verse's median is 12 bars in every decade (8, 12 and 16 all common); a chorus 8; a bridge 8; a solo 8 to 9.
- **What a song has**: a chorus in 70% (1960s), 85% (1970s), 94% (1980s); a bridge in 30 to 45%; an instrumental solo in 28%
  (1960s), 35% (1970s), 45% (1980s); a pre-chorus in 7%, 12%, 23%; an interlude in 21%, 28%, 37%.
- **Endings**: a fade in more than half of all songs, 56% (1960s) to 69% (1980s). An outro section in about four in ten, its
  median 8 bars (1960s), 12 (1970s), 16 (1980s). Ending cold on a chorus or verse is the minority, commonest early.
- **Size**: median 80 bars and 8 sections (1960s), 94 and 9 (1970s), 112 and 11 (1980s).
