# Melody

## What a Melody Is Made Of

- **Contour**: the shape over a phrase. The common shapes are an arch, a descent, a rise to a late peak, and a level line with one
  excursion. A phrase usually has one high point, and a section usually has one highest note, saved for where it matters. (practice)
- **Motion**: mostly steps, with a few leaps. A leap is commonly followed by a step back the other way, filling the gap it opened.
  Large leaps upward carry effort and lift; downward ones release. (practice)
- **Rhythm**: a small stock of rhythmic cells, repeated. A melody is recognized by its rhythm with the pitches changed more readily
  than by its pitches with the rhythm changed. Notes that start off the beat push; long notes on strong beats settle. (practice)
- **Range**: a sung tune stays within about an octave to a tenth; an instrument's tune ranges wider and may run longer without
  breath. (practice)
- **Chord tones and the notes between**: notes on strong beats mostly belong to the chord of the moment, and the notes between pass
  or lean. A note that leans on a strong beat and then resolves by step is where much of a tune's feeling comes from. (practice)

## Phrases

- Melodies come in phrases of two or four bars with breath between, often paired as a question that ends away from home and an
  answer that ends on it. (practice)
- Four phrases commonly follow AABA, AABB, ABAB or AAAB by their material: enough return to be learned, one change to stay
  interesting. (practice)
- Where a phrase ends matters as much as its notes: ending on the home note closes, ending on another chord tone leaves the door
  open, ending early leaves room for an answer from another player. (practice)

## Ways to Make One

- **From a motif**: a cell of three to five notes, then repeated, moved to another step of the scale (sequence), turned upside
  down, stretched, shortened, or given a new ending. Most durable tunes are a little material worked hard. (practice)
- **From the chords**: outline each chord's tones and join them by steps; good for bass-led and riff-led music. (practice)
- **From a rhythm**: fix a spoken or clapped rhythm first, as the words would give, then find pitches for it. (practice)
- **From a scale walk**: step along the scale from a chosen note, turning at chosen points, with rules for when to leap. (practice)
- **From a row or a rule**: an ordered set of pitches or a process, for music that avoids a key on purpose. (practice)
- **By answering**: write the second phrase as a reply to the first, same rhythm with a different ending, or the same ending
  reached another way. (practice)

## Ways to Keep One Interesting

- Change one thing at a time: keep the rhythm and move the pitches, or keep the pitches and shift the rhythm.
- Start phrases at different points in the bar: on the beat, before it as a pickup, or after it.
- Save the highest note, the longest note and the biggest leap, and do not spend them twice in a section.
- Let the tune rest; a gap is where another player answers and where the listener catches up.
- Verse low, narrow and busy; chorus higher, wider and longer-noted. A bridge takes a new rhythm or starts on a new chord.
- Ornaments, used sparingly: a grace note, a slide into a note, a turn, a held note that swells. (all practice)

## Phrasing A Line

How a singer or a wind player delivers a line, which a tune written by rule lacks. (from a review of the Phrasing generator's design; practice)

- Phrasing has three pillars: breath, a loudness contour, and articulation. Breath alone is the thinnest of the three.
- **The goal note.** A line grows toward one note and relaxes after it. The goal is usually a long note on a strong beat late in the
  line, not simply the highest note: the highest note may be a short passing note, sit on a weak beat or come first. A simple rule:
  the longest note on a strong beat in the line's second half, the higher and then the later where two tie. The rise is slower than
  the fall, the range is modest, and the line's last note is its softest.
- **Metric accent.** Strong beats are a little louder than weak ones underneath the contour; without that the contour sounds like a fader.
- **The first note after a breath** is clean and a little louder.
- **Breath.** A performer with no room to breathe shortens the line's last note first, then steals time from the note before, and
  only at the last drops a short ornamental note on a weak beat; the arrival note is never dropped. Breaths are not all one size:
  a short catch breath, an eighth to a quarter, between the two lines of a pair, a fuller one, a quarter to a half, after the pair,
  and the longest at a section's end. A breath scales with tempo; six eighths is a hole, not a breath. A fixed alternation of two
  sizes is heard as a machine's pattern.
- **Articulation.** Legato connects the notes inside a line, but a repeated pitch is always struck again, with a gap; the line's
  last note is shortened and softened in every manner; played detached, long notes keep nearly their full length and only short
  notes are clipped.
- **A hierarchy of lines.** A line of four bars is two of two, with a smaller dip at its middle.
- **Anticipation**, a note an eighth early, suits syncopated popular styles and is wrong for others.
- With notes alone (start, length, loudness) a held note cannot swell or fade; that needs a controller.
- **More on the breath** (from a second review, which read the generator's code). A breath is musical time that scales with the
  tempo, never a count of eighths, and takes at most half of the line's last note; where the last note is too short, time comes
  from the note before, or no breath is taken. Where the tune already rests, no breath is cut. Pickup notes before a bar line
  belong to the next line, so the breath goes before them. The breath after the last line of a clip is the fullest.
- **What matters most** in making a programmed tune sound played: first the loudness shape of each line, since notes all at one
  loudness are the clearest sign of a machine; then how notes end and join. Timing nudges matter least. An early note is chosen
  where it fits, a long note before a strong beat, never by a count of lines, and is off unless the style is syncopated.
- Shaping a line (its loudness and its joining) is a different thing from a ramp over a whole clip or section: the first knows
  where lines end.

## How a Writer Thinks About a Song's Melodies

From a review asked for before the song wizard's melody panels were redrawn. (practice)

- A song has several melodies, one for each kind of section: the verse's, the chorus's, the pre-chorus's, the bridge's. These are
  different melodies, each written for contrast. "The same melody developed" means a later verse against the first, a last chorus
  lifted, or an instrument stating the verse's melody. A countermelody is a second, lesser line against the main one; a solo is a
  freer line over a section's chords. Nobody thinks of "the melody" as one object with one setting.
- What a writer decides, most important first: who carries it; where the hook sits; how busy it is and where it rests; how high
  and how wide it goes, the highest note once, in the chorus; how much it repeats; and, for experts only, how it sits on the chords.
- Decided once for the song: who carries it, the overall range, the character of the line, the general busyness, how much the
  sections share. Decided for a section: its height against the others, its busyness, where its peak falls, how much it repeats,
  and whether the carrier rests or hands the melody to another player.
- The sections relate: the chorus higher, on longer notes, simpler and more repeated; the verse lower, busier and narrower; the
  pre-chorus climbing; the bridge somewhere new. A shared motif often ties them.
- With no singer the melody is the head or theme, more distinctive in rhythm, and often passed between players. A riff is not a
  melody to players: short, repeated, low or middle, part of the groove. A riff may still be the song's identity.
- What a person does with a written melody, in order of worth: hear it, alone and with the band; ask for another version of one
  section's; keep a phrase and rewrite around it; change who plays it or its octave; edit notes by hand, which few do.
- A program should not ask about intervals, scale degrees, shares of chord notes or contours drawn bar by bar.

## What Makes a Melody Good, and Why a Written One Sounds Like a Machine

- In order of weight: a rhythm that repeats and is known when clapped; a motif of two to five notes that returns a little
  changed; each phrase an arch, a rise or a fall, and the section's highest note once, about two thirds through, on a strong beat;
  phrase endings that read as question, off the home note, then answer; phrases a singer can breathe between, not all one length;
  notes of the chord on strong beats and long notes, thirds and fifths more than roots; mostly steps, a leap past a fourth followed
  by a step back; a note outside the chord on a strong beat resolved by step; a range of about a tenth. Rules hold all of these and
  give a melody that is not bad; only the ear picks a good one. (practice)
- A melody sounds made by a machine when it wanders, so each phrase's last note and peak are chosen first and written toward;
  when every phrase has one length and density; when each line draws a new rhythm, so one rhythm cell is drawn for a section and
  used in most of its lines; when it has no motif; when every line peaks; when lines land on roots, which belong at final endings;
  when the only silence is at line ends; and when leaps are random, where one leap a phrase is the phrase's feature. Walking by
  step makes scales: the motif comes first, the plan second, and steps only fill between planned notes. (practice)
- A song is one melodic design, not a melody for each kind of section: the chorus states the hook's motif whole, the verse has
  another motif and may borrow one small feature of the hook once, late, the pre-chorus repeats a fragment that shortens and
  rises, and the bridge is the contrast. A verse belongs to its chorus by family and is its opposite in behaviour: lower, busier,
  open-ended, starting off the beat where the chorus starts on it. (practice)
- What returns is not identical: the chorus is nearly the same each time, as is the pre-chorus, whose sameness says the chorus is
  coming; a later verse keeps its contour and endings with a pickup changed, a few notes added or dropped and a last line that
  leads on; the last chorus is lifted, by an extension, a higher last statement or a key rise; a harmony voice joins the hook
  from the second chorus; the song's last line lands on the home note and holds. Small random changes each time are noise. (practice)

## The Objects of a Song's Melodies, and the Order They Are Written In

- The smallest thing is a motif, a rhythm and a contour of three to seven notes, the rhythm the more memorable half; a song has
  one or a few, and a line names the motif it comes from and how it was changed. A kind of section has one master melody of
  lines. A chorus has a hook, which is a line built from the first motif, a placement saying which lines are the hook, and body
  lines that answer the hook and lead back to it. Each time a section returns is an occurrence: its master with a recipe of
  changes, not a copy, until a person edits it. The song has a register plan: verse low to middle, pre-chorus rising, chorus
  highest, bridge elsewhere, and no kind trespasses. (practice)
- The most memorable thing is written first and the rest serves it: the frame (key, range, register plan, chords, form), the
  hook, the chorus's body, the verse, the pre-chorus, the bridge, the riff and a second hook, then the variations. The body
  takes the hook's rhythm, peak and last note; the verse takes the chorus's peak, to stay under, and its density, to differ; the
  pre-chorus takes the verse's last note and the chorus's first; the bridge takes all of it, to avoid. Where a riff leads, the
  riff is second and the singing leaves it room. Where there is no chorus, the refrain line is the hook, the verse's last line.
  Where a cell loops, the cell is the hook and the sections are thinner and fuller dressings of it. (practice)
- What makes a written melody good is repetition and shape across lines, held by rules that are not dials: the order of the
  peaks, the contrast of density, the plan of the endings, where in the bar a line starts, the recovery of a leap, chord notes on
  strong beats. Dials of note-to-note motion are seasoning. Rhythm deserves more of the design than pitch. (practice)
- A genre is a preset of one writer: its values, its way of writing each kind of section, its vocabulary of rhythms, its scale
  habits and its register plan; never a second, simpler writer. (practice)
- The ways a return differs: the same melody with new pickups, the last two notes and the peak of each line kept; a long note
  split or two joined, never in the hook; the last line raised; the hook's peak lifted, in the last chorus; the last line or the
  hook said again and the last note lengthened; a harmony voice a third or a sixth away, from the second chorus; the first half
  thinned; a pre-chorus halved. A variation never changes the hook's rhythm: choruses change by dressing, verses by notes. To vary
  notes a person wrote, the phrase ends, each line's peak and ending note, the repeated rhythm and the range are found first, and
  shown, so that a wrong reading is corrected. (practice)
- With no words a line still needs a count of syllables and a pattern of stresses, or a verse does not sound sung. (practice)
