# Band Interplay

- A band is a few roles: the **tune** (voice or lead instrument), the **answer** (whoever fills beside the tune), the **chords**,
  the **bass**, and the **time** (drums and percussion). One player may hold two roles, and a role may pass between players from
  section to section. (practice)
- **One thing in front at a time.** Whoever has the tune is given room: others play less, lower, softer or longer notes while the
  tune moves, and move when the tune rests. (practice)
- **Call and answer**: the answer comes in the gap at the end of the tune's line, begins after the line's last note has landed, and
  is out of the way before the next line. An answer can be a fill (short, some lines), a reply (every line, as in the blues and
  gospel), a doubling of the hook, or a riff that runs under the whole section and is the song's second hook. (practice)
- A player who answers varies what is played: the same fill note for note at every return sounds like a machine, and answers
  usually grow busier as a song builds. (practice)
- **Doubling** thickens: the tune in octaves or in unison on a second instrument, the bass with the kick, a riff on guitar and
  bass together. **Harmonizing**, a second line a third or a sixth away, sweetens; twin lead instruments in harmony is a rock sound
  of its own. (practice)
- **Registers**: each player keeps a band of pitch. Bass low, chords in the middle, tune above, with the answer either above the
  tune or in its gaps. Two parts in one band of pitch with different rhythms blur. (practice)
- **Layering through a song**: add a part at each new section, or take one away. A first verse may be voice, one chord instrument
  and light drums; the last chorus everything. A breakdown, most players out, makes the return larger than adding ever could.
  (practice)
- **Intensity** is not loudness alone: it is how many play, how busy each is, how high, how hard the drums hit, which cymbals, and
  how much the players are locked to one rhythm. A section's intensity scales how much of each player's ability is used; it does
  not change who the player is. (judgment)
- **Trading**: players swap the front, four or eight bars each, in solos; the band reacts to a soloist, dropping down as a solo
  starts and rising with it. (practice)
- In an **instrumental**, the lead instrument takes the tune and plays as an instrument can: wider, longer runs, more variety, and
  the answering role moves to another player. Backup voices go. (practice)
- **Starts and endings** are interplay too: who enters first and who leaves last says what the song is built on, groove or riff.
  See `SONG_STRUCTURE.md`.

## House Bands and Session Players

Three studio systems of the sixties, each a fixed group of players who made a label's or a city's sound. What they share: parts
invented on the spot from a bare chart, each player owning one narrow job, and nobody playing over anyone else. (practice)

- **The Detroit house band.** Songs came as chord charts with the writers' ideas; the players filled in the rest. Roles: a
  **bass** that is the lead instrument of the rhythm section, melodic, syncopated, full of chromatic passing notes, seldom the same
  bar twice; **two drummers** on some dates, or one with a second player on tambourine, the backbeat reinforced by handclaps, foot
  stomps or a snare on all four beats; **three guitars**, each with one job: one chopping a dry chord on 2 and 4 high on the neck,
  one doubling the piano's or the bass's line, one playing fills and figures; **piano and organ** together; **vibraphone and
  tambourine** for the top; strings and horns arranged afterward. The density works because every part is small.
- **The Memphis house band.** No arrangers and no charts: the four players and the horn players worked the song out by trying
  accents, bass lines and chord inversions until it sat. The **drummer** places 2 and 4 late and deep and plays almost no fills;
  the **bass** locks to the kick with short repeated figures and leaves the delayed backbeat alone; the **guitar** plays two- or
  three-note chords and fills only in the singer's gaps, and often doubles the bass an octave up for a riff; the **organ or
  piano** holds terse chords; the **horns** play unison lines, punches and held pads as a head arrangement, in place of backing
  singers. Fewer notes than any other style; the space is the sound.
- **The Nashville session system.** Three songs in a three-hour session, heard for the first time at its start; the players write
  a number chart by ear and build a head arrangement in minutes. Roles: an upright **bass** on roots and fifths, doubled note for
  note by a muted six-string bass guitar an octave up with a pick, for a click on each note; **drums** light, brushes or a
  cross-stick, often no cymbals; an acoustic **rhythm guitar** strumming high like a snare; a **lead guitar** and a **steel
  guitar** (or fiddle) who trade: one takes the intro, the other the turnaround, each fills in alternate lines of the verse and
  never together; a **piano** playing fills with a grace note sliding up a tone into a chord tone, the top note held; a vocal
  group singing soft held chords, and strings on the smoother records.
- **Number charts.** Chords are written as scale degrees so that a song is played in any key at once. Each number is a bar; two
  numbers underlined share a bar; a diamond is a chord struck once and let ring; a mark over a number is a push, the chord
  arriving an eighth early; a slash names a bass note; a minor chord has a dash. The chart says who takes the intro, the
  turnaround and the solo, where the band stops, and where a "walk" joins two chords. Everything else is the player's style: the
  chart is the same for every instrument, and each reads it into its own job.
- **What to take from all three**: give each player one job and a register; pass the fills around by turn, line by line; let one
  instrument only answer the singer at a time; settle who has the intro and the turnaround; diamonds, stops and pushes placed
  together by the whole band are what make a rhythm section sound arranged.

## Trading Parts

A note Claude wrote in another conversation, kept nearly whole, names and all.
(practice throughout)

Players trade parts in two ways: they pass the lead back and forth in agreed-size chunks, or they swap roles, so whoever was
accompanying steps forward and the former lead drops back into support.

### Trading in Fixed Chunks

The classic form is trading fours in jazz. A soloist plays four bars, the drummer answers alone for four, and this repeats around
the form. A few things make it work:

- The form keeps running. The chords continue underneath, even during the drum breaks, when nobody is playing them. Everyone
  counts silently, and the test of a band is whether they all land together on the top of the next chorus.
- Each answer responds to the last phrase. A player echoes the rhythm, finishes the thought, tops it or deliberately contrasts it.
- The chunks often shrink. Eights become fours, then twos, then ones, which raises the excitement because the handoffs come
  faster. The same idea as a drum fill speeding up its subdivision.

Other traditions use the same structure:

- Blues trades between the voice and the guitar: two bars of vocal, two bars of answer.
- Bluegrass passes "breaks" around the band, with each instrument taking the melody for one pass.
- Indian classical music has sawal-jawab (question and answer) between the soloist and the tabla, also with shrinking phrases.
- Rock has guitar duels and twin-lead harmonies.

### Swapping Roles

When the lead changes hands, everyone else adjusts:

- The new soloist starts low, in energy and often in register, leaving room to build. The rhythm section drops down to match.
- The old soloist becomes an accompanist, or lays out entirely for a chorus to clear the texture.
- Compers change their approach to suit the instrument. A pianist comps differently behind a saxophone than behind a guitar,
  because the registers clash differently.
- In a bass solo, the drummer switches to light cymbals or brushes, the comping thins to almost nothing, and someone else may
  cover the bass role.

### Signaling the Handoff

Since trades often are not written down, bands rely on cues:

- **Convention**: a standard solo order, or a number of choruses agreed beforehand.
- **Musical cues**: the soloist winds down with a phrase that sounds like an ending, drops in register, or quotes the melody to
  signal "last chorus".
- **Physical cues**: eye contact, a nod, lifting the instrument, or pointing at the head to mean "back to the tune".
- **Overlap**: the outgoing soloist often plays into the first beat of the next section, and the new soloist may pick up the
  last idea as a starting point, so the handoff sounds continuous.

### Trading Within a Texture

Some trading is built into the part itself. Hocket is the extreme case, with a melody split note by note. Balinese gamelan interlocks two
players into one fast line, and funk guitarists split one rhythm part between them. Stride pianists do it alone, with the hands
trading bass and chord.

## A Riff Belongs to the Song

- A band thinks of a riff as the song's, usually one, sometimes a second for a bridge, and each player has a role toward it: plays
  it, doubles it an octave below or above, plays hits on its accents, or stays out; the drums put the kick on its accents.
  Nobody is taught a riff: doubling is a role. (practice)
- Under singing a riff simplifies: fewer notes, lower, muted, or its first half only; it is at full strength in the intro, between
  the singer's lines and after the chorus. By section it is full, simplified, stripped to one player, or doubled by everyone, with
  a turnaround in its last bar every four or eight bars. It follows the chords, moved with each or held while the bass moves, and
  lasts one, two or four bars. (practice)
- A riff has a relation to the hook: it is the hook, it answers the hook, or it stays under the hook. (practice)
